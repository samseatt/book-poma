## Opening vignette

From that unsettled September in 2026, I let myself imagine an ordinary morning two and a half years ahead.

March 11, 2029. I am at home in Calgary, where spring remains a rumour circulating among optimistic shrubs.

The agents working with me have spent the night revising part of our little civic experiment, testing it and leaving a working version for inspection. Work that once kept several people occupied now proceeds while one of them sleeps. I am adjusting rather well to being that one.

Elsewhere, agents are negotiating deliveries, resolving claims and carrying administrative work through several organizations without requiring someone to supervise every click. The gains are substantial. So is the rearrangement of livelihoods around them. The argument about whether AI can do the work increasingly concerns who gets paid when it does.

Then a neighbourhood message arrives.

*Mom’s refill is ready at Mahogany Shoppers. Delivery cancelled again. Can anyone collect it?*

An overnight update has broken a service used to authorize automated bookings. Most things still work, which makes the exceptions harder to recognize. Some requests have been rerouted successfully; others continue to display confirmations issued before the arrangements fell apart. My assistant checks the underlying records and catches the discrepancy. It can explain exactly where the handoff failed.

The medicine remains at the pharmacy.

I think of my father. Three years since the March morning that began his final decline. I have spent much of the intervening time thinking about what it would take for help to survive its passage through a system. Here is a modest examination of the subject, arriving before I have finished breakfast.

By late morning I am at the community hall. A few of us have been testing the coordination tools I hope to have ready by September. Today has volunteered to test them for us.

The room is familiar: folding tables, wet boots, coffee brewed under an expansive interpretation of the word. A nurse practitioner is checking requests that need clinical judgment. A retired electrician has acquired a small audience around a power controller. Two teenagers are maintaining the shared board, occasionally consulting the adults on matters outside the teenagers’ considerable jurisdiction.

Their agents do much of the clerical work. Requests arrive through different services and in different languages; duplicate offers are reconciled, routes adjusted, cancellations carried back to everyone affected. The shared board shows what has actually been accepted and what is still waiting. Personal details stay with the people who need them.

It is an impressive amount of machinery to have available in a room hired by the hour.

The pharmacy request is still waiting.

The daughter has arranged permission for someone to collect the prescription. A volunteer who seemed available has withdrawn: his own mother needs him. The system finds another possibility.

Me.

I inspect the suggestion with the sudden rigor of a man whose principles have located his afternoon.

There is a free hour in my calendar. There is also work I had intended to do in it. The assistant asks whether I want to take the pickup; it has learned that empty space in a calendar is not necessarily abandoned property.

I accept.

The arrangement is confirmed with the daughter and pharmacy. I receive the details I need. The board changes from *waiting* to *accepted*, leaving delivery conspicuously unfinished.

For a moment I am tempted to call the problem solved. Then I have to find my keys.

Before leaving, I notice a proposed feature on the teenagers’ screen: a score for volunteer reliability. Sensible enough, until I think of the man who has just withdrawn to care for his mother.

“Where would his reason go?”

One of them opens the record. There is a field for the cancellation, none for the other obligation.

We leave the score out.

I head for the pharmacy with a small task and a slightly less complete theory.

## The First Knot

What interests me in that imagined morning is how much capacity already exists. The medicine is ready. There are vehicles, willing people and machines capable of arranging most of the journey. Yet a need can remain stranded among them.

The missing connection contains more than information. Someone must be entitled to ask, someone able to agree, and someone responsible for noticing whether the agreement has become an event. Intelligence can help establish those connections. It cannot make my spare hour belong to a stranger, or relieve a pharmacist of responsibility because the request arrives beautifully formatted.

This complicates the builder’s pleasure. I can see the efficiency of joining everything up. I can also see how quickly my useful map could become another institution through which a person must plead to be understood.

We have met that temptation before. The basket gathers enough people to carry a burden; eventually, somebody begins deciding what everyone else must carry. The arrangement acquires a custodian, the custodian acquires discretion, and departure becomes surprisingly difficult.

Renewal has to contend with that inheritance. We begin among existing loyalties and obligations, some protective, some exhausted, many doing both jobs at once. A humane design needs enough continuity to remain trustworthy and enough freedom to discover that an old requirement has outlived its purpose. Faster administration can preserve the requirement indefinitely.

That is why I want the first experiment small enough to argue with. A person should be able to correct it, refuse it, or reveal something its designers failed to imagine. Even the cancellation must retain its capacity to teach us.

The pharmacy run gives these questions a local address. History gives them a longer record. People have been gathering scattered capacities into collective power for a very long time. We tend to remember the person who eventually stood at the centre.

I want to look at what was happening before he got there.
