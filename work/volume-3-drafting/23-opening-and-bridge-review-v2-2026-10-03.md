## Opening vignette

From that battered autumn of 2026, I let myself imagine a Sunday morning in Calgary: March 11, 2029. In this version of the future, I have given myself the morning off. I am trying to be realistic, but there are limits.

I want a walk and a coffee. Preferably in that order, so the walk can pass as a health decision.

Calgary has begun to thaw in small, untrustworthy patches. Alberta, meanwhile, has put its clocks permanently on summer time. It is a modest achievement in the governance of nature: we have settled the hour, and are still negotiating the temperature.

My assistant has spent the night untangling an insurance claim and arranging the return of something I should not have bought. It handles the correspondence, checks the refund and leaves me the exception that needs my say. Whole stretches of the old administrative life now happen this way, machine talking to machine while the supposed beneficiary gets on with being alive. I approve the remaining decision and close the screen before it finds another way to improve me.

Then I notice the date again.

Three years since the March morning that began my father's final decline. I can send the calendar forward as far as I like; it will keep bringing that day with it. Even here, in a future I am free to furnish, I cannot put him back in the room.

A walk, then.

I am looking for my other glove when a neighbourhood request appears.

*Mom's prescription is ready at Mahogany Shoppers. Her delivery's been cancelled. I'm in Vancouver. Is anyone going that way?*

I am not going that way. I have not yet established which way I am going. This is a vulnerable position from which to defend one's plans.

A booking service has failed that morning. My assistant has already checked the pharmacy's message against the cancelled delivery and found that the prescription is still waiting. It can arrange the handoff if I agree to collect it. The useful part happens quietly; there is no need for a demonstration of intelligence while a woman waits for her medication.

I say yes. The daughter confirms the arrangement with the pharmacy. A name and an address arrive. The diagnosis does not. I put on my coat.

The tariff fights and separation arguments of 2026 come back to me as I step outside. We have spent several years discussing who could afford to separate from whom. This morning, I am trying to establish who can pick something up for someone else.

The pavement glitters in places where it would be wiser not to trust it. I shorten my stride. After all the intelligence we have added to the world, I am still negotiating directly with a thin layer of frozen water.

At the pharmacy, the arrangements hold. The pharmacist checks what needs checking and hands me a small paper bag. There is no dramatic rescue. I have become a delivery service with unusually strong opinions about civilization.

Outside, I tuck the bag inside my coat to keep it warm. The gesture catches me off guard. It is such a small thing to be able to do for somebody's parent.

The woman who opens the door is looking past me.

“Is it icy?”

“In places.”

“I told her.”

Her daughter is on a screen on the kitchen counter, still in Vancouver and apparently still losing the argument. I hold up the bag. She thanks me; her mother takes it and tells her to get on with her Sunday. The daughter begins one more instruction. The mother ends the call with the practiced timing of someone who has raised this person and considers the matter substantially complete.

“Coffee?”

I have, technically, reached my original destination.

She waves me in. I leave my wet boots by the door.

“Milk?”

“Black is fine.”

“Good. I'm out.”

We sit at the kitchen table. She asks which street I live on. I discover that I have passed her house often enough to recognize the garden, but have somehow kept the gardener theoretical. There is a pot on the windowsill with a few green shoots leaning toward the light. She says it is too early to put anything outside. We look out at Calgary, which is making the same point less politely.

For a while, we talk about ordinary things.

I had wanted to be alone with the thought of my father. Instead, I am drinking coffee with somebody else's mother. She pushes the sugar toward me, although I have declined it, and returns to her account of the neighbour's dog.

I let her finish.

## The First Knot

I would like some of the future to feel like that: time recovered from machinery and returned to a life whose next use has not already been assigned.

The temptation will be to fill every released hour. We are very accomplished at this. Give us a tool that saves ten minutes and we will soon have an arrangement that requires eleven. The Still Arrow can acquire extraordinary speed while the person carrying it remains late for something.

That imagined morning interests me because the technology does useful work and then leaves room. The medicine still depends on laboratories, manufacturing, a pharmacist's judgment and an arrangement someone can trust. My little contribution travels through all of that. The coffee belongs to the woman who offers it.

There is room here for a form of renewal that our usual measurements barely notice. A stranger becomes a neighbour. Someone far away can stop worrying for a while. An hour becomes available for something that was never on the calendar. These are small gains, but a civilization ought to be capable of making room for them without requiring either a crisis or a saint.

It also has to make help dependable. Occasional kindness cannot carry the whole weight of an absent service. Nor should the willing person become the permanent remedy for everybody else's neglected obligations. Generosity needs somewhere to stand without being put in harness.

This is the difficulty inside our longing to belong. We need one another, and we need some say in what that need entitles others to ask of us. A connection can give us strength; it can also give someone a handle. Carney's warning at Davos in 2026 concerned that danger at the scale of nations. The question follows us home.

How do we become more capable together without making ourselves somebody's property?

History usually introduces the man who has acquired an army and a sufficiently flattering portrait. I would like to begin a little earlier, while people are still deciding what they owe one another—and who, if anyone, gets to collect.

## Editorial and factual notes — outside proposed prose

- **Scope:** A personal Sunday walk, errand and visit replace the community-hall building scene. The proposed prose remains conjectural from its opening sentence. No new factual autobiographical episode, remembered dialogue or specific event from the father's care has been invented. Future weather, dialogue, neighbours, service failure and household arrangements are fictional scenario details.
- **Length:** 1,118 words for the two proposed sections, compared with approximately 1,543 in the canonical opening and bridge. The new version spends more space on the encounter and emotional movement than the first review version.
- **Division of labour:** Reserve the prior experiment, coordination-room and software/volunteer-score material for Interlude 23's later revision. It has not yet been transplanted. A shared request can connect the chapter's lived experience to the interlude's examination of the machinery, without replaying the whole scene.
- **Continuity on adoption:** The current Chapter 23 still refers to the community hall, teenagers, electrician and nurse practitioner later in its design prose and ending. Those callbacks will need deliberate reconciliation if this opening is adopted. This review does not silently rewrite those later sections or the interlude.
- **Calendar:** 11 March 2029 is a Sunday, checked with the Gregorian calendar. It is not the father's death anniversary; the draft correctly refers to the March event preceding his later death.
- **Alberta clocks:** The [Alberta government's time policy](https://www.alberta.ca/albertas-new-time-system-abt), checked 3 October 2026, says the province will retain permanent Alberta Time (UTC−6, formerly MDT) from November 2026. The scene assumes that policy continues in 2029. It does not invent a March 2029 clock change. “Summer time” is the narrator's informal description of the retained daylight-time offset.
- **Political memory:** The 2026 independence initiative is documented by [Elections Alberta](https://www.elections.ab.ca/resources/media/news-releases/new-citizen-initiative-petition-issued-2/). The scene remembers the debate without specifying its later outcome. The author's welcomed separation/errand line is retained with tense adjusted to this present-tense vignette.
- **Davos:** [Carney's 20 January 2026 address](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/speeches/2026/01/20/principled-and-pragmatic-canadas-path-prime-minister-carney-addresses) discusses economic integration used as coercion, tariffs and dependence, alongside the value of cooperation. The domestic analogy in the bridge is the author's interpretation, not a quotation or a claim that household relationships and international relations are equivalent. The fuller Iran/geopolitical/climate/Nepal/9–11 account remains Theta work.
- **AI:** Routine delegation across services extrapolates from [NIST's 2026 agent initiative](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/02/announcing-ai-agent-standards-initiative-interoperable-and-secure) and its documented interoperability constraints. Household task success, provider failure and adoption in this specific 2029 setting remain scenario choices, not forecasts certified by the source. The assistant competently verifies and arranges the pickup; the narrator does not become a builder in the scene.
