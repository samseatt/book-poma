## Alice, Down the Rabbit Hole: Seeing Like a State

*The twist, in one line: a state can govern only what it can see, it sees by simplifying people into categories, and its errors fall hardest on the people its categories don't fit.*

In 1998, the political scientist James Scott published a book with an unforgettable opening. In the eighteenth century, Prussian and Saxon foresters, wanting to calculate the timber their forests could yield, reimagined the forest as a set of countable trees. Then they replanted it to match the count: straight rows of a single species, the undergrowth cleared, everything that didn't produce timber removed. The first generation of these "scientific forests" was a triumph. The second generation sickened and died, because the life the foresters had cleared away (the fungi, the insects, the tangle of species) had been what kept the forest alive.[^v1-c8-scott]

Scott's argument was that states do the same to people. To tax, conscript, educate and police a population, a state must first make it *legible*: it invents permanent surnames, standard measures, property maps, censuses, identity documents and passports, and then acts on the simplified picture as if it were the world. Legibility is not wicked; it's how a state of millions functions at all, the same compression Chapter 7 found in a price. A census is a nation's memory, compressed. But the compressed picture always throws something away, and the state's errors fall hardest on the people who don't fit its categories.

In this book's terms, a state is a classifier. It takes in the full, tangled reality of a person and outputs a label: citizen, resident, visitor, threat. Chapter 4 warned that every classifier lives on a threshold between accepting the center too quickly and debating the surround forever, and that a system checking its labels only against its own past labels closes its loop. Interlude 4 found machines trained on our approval doing the same. A border is where that classifier meets you in person, at a booth, under fluorescent light, with the power to say no. Most of the time, the classifier is right about the pattern. The question this chapter keeps circling is what it owes the people it's wrong about.
