## The wager worth keeping

A collection of people can acquire capacities that none of its members possesses alone. Can we deliberately strengthen that collective capacity while preserving each person's ability to dissent, withdraw and correct the collective? Could assistance at the interfaces between people change the scale at which this becomes possible?

That preserves the author's ambition: collective emergence, not merely agreeable meetings. It also leaves an honest opening for the stronger question of a collective subject. Functional agency and phenomenal experience require different arguments; neither should be prematurely defined away.

I would give Chapter 5 the human question and Interlude 5 the construction problem. Alice asks what makes a collective capable of acting and accepting responsibility. Bob can ask what an IIT-inspired, causally explicit account of a human–agent network might require. The existing incoming Bob already has useful material on civics, the protocol hourglass and a mosaic of local forms. The author's preferred Φ-like inquiry can deepen that material rather than be displaced by it.

## Giving the crisis idea legs

There is evidence for the first step. John Drury's [2018 review of mass emergencies](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2018.1471948) describes how perceived common fate can generate shared identity, mutual support, expectations of help and coordination among strangers. The research challenges the assumption that disaster automatically produces selfish panic. It does not show that every crisis creates solidarity, or establish the timing of durable accountability relative to coordination.

The useful hypothesis is therefore narrower than “coordination comes before responsibility.” Helping a stranger already expresses responsibility. What may lag is an **enduring arrangement for authorizing action, distributing costs, remembering obligations and correcting failures**. Those capacities cannot be inferred from people moving together for an hour. List and Pettit's [account of organized group agency](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199591565.001.0001) provides one foothold; Sampson and colleagues' [collective-efficacy study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.277.5328.918) supplies another. Neither proves the proposed temporal gap.

A possible research question: after a common shock, which new ties and expectations persist once immediate danger recedes? Follow actual cooperation, who can authorize commitments, whether burdens are accepted or disputed, and what happens when promises fail. Compare groups with similar exposure but different pre-existing organizations and resources. Do not count the duration of a chat group as the duration of solidarity.

## The stronger integration question for Bob

IIT measures proposed intrinsic causal organization, not a count of connections, correlations, messages, or people agreeing. Applying [IIT 4.0](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011465) literally requires a defensible specification of units, states, transitions, interventions, partitions and temporal grain. A social-network graph alone supplies none of that. Treating each person as a node also raises a serious coarse-graining question: which causal capacities survive that compression?

There is still something worth investigating. For a defined collective task, compare what the coupled group can accomplish with what matched separated subgroups can accomplish. Then perturb the bridges, the shared information and the decision rules separately. Preserve equivalent resources where possible. This can test whether the benefit comes from interaction, common input, one indispensable broker, or mere pooling of independent answers. Call the result a task-specific measure of collective capacity until an actual argument licenses more. It is not automatically Φ or evidence of a shared experience.

The extra step worth pursuing is **integration that increases shared capability without making the collective brittle or its members captive**. Maximum coupling may be the wrong objective: tightly linked systems can transmit errors, suppress independent evidence and fail together. Useful integration may need selective boundaries, redundancy and protected disagreement. That tension is promising precisely because “more connection” cannot answer it.

The author's Neuma/MOxAIC possibility belongs here as a design proposal: assistance might carry context across boundaries, surface misunderstandings and maintain explicit obligations, allowing people to cooperate beyond unaided personal acquaintance. Whether it expands meaningful relational capacity, merely delegates administration, or makes an intermediary dominant remains open. Measure human time and burden, quality of correction, autonomy and recovery when the intermediary fails—not just network size or task throughput. AI assistance has not been shown to raise a universal Dunbar ceiling, and that ceiling itself is disputed.

## A possible opening, not an adopted replacement

*The twist, in one line: a society may learn to move together before it learns what its members have agreed to carry together.*

Chapter 4 left me wondering who gets to decide what counts as evidence inside a person. A society makes the question less private. My correction can arrive at your door looking very much like an insult.

Los Angeles was full of people bound tightly to other people. The difficulty was getting those bonds to do useful work across the gaps. A fraternity could arrange a treasure hunt across half the city while remaining largely incurious about the street outside its own door. We had demonstrated considerable organizational ability. Nobody had specified the range.

A catastrophe can alter that range. Strangers may become a “we” quickly enough to carry one another out of danger. But after the carrying comes another problem: who has promised what, who gets heard, and who is still responsible when the rescuers go home? I wonder whether a society can learn to make that transition deliberately, before it needs the fire.

This sample introduces the question; it does not replace the email scene or settle the more ambitious integration conjecture. The existing three-person-house scene should do much of the later work, with no explanatory tail required.
