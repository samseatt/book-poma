OVERTURE TO VOLUME II

A Nagging Stillness

> "In order to understand the world, one has to turn away from it on occasion." --- **Albert Camus**[^v2-overture-camus]

A man crossed a hotel lobby in São Paulo's Berrini business district well after midnight, beside a woman he called Princess. Outside, the city had switched to dark mode for the night: restaurants closed, the towers blank behind their glass, the sidewalks so empty you could have counted the cracks. Only the hotel stayed awake (polished floors, bright ceiling panels, a faint smell of citrus, and a night clerk who had seen late arrivals before).

In his hand was a phone that was more corpse than device.

He had spent the day trying to do something simple: celebrate a birthday in a city whose language he didn't speak, one of those birthdays that are supposed to mean something. He had pictured candles and a real dinner somewhere local, not out of a guidebook. He had prepared the modern way: a few pins on a map, half a plan, and faith that the network would hold and the battery would last.

The battery did not last. It didn't fail dramatically (no flames, no leap into traffic); it simply declined, steadily and professionally, much like its owner. He had come in on a long flight and two overnight buses, and was running on street food, late hours and a few drinks too many. He wandered up and down Avenida Paulista, walking, checking his phone, waiting and checking again, while the battery drifted toward zero.

He had asked Princess to bring his charger when her conference ended. It seemed a small request; a bit of plastic and wire shouldn't decide a night. But in a city where you can't improvise in the local language, a phone is your translator, navigator, memory and coordinator. When it dies, your options go with it, and you're left with whatever patterns a tired mind can still run on its own.

She arrived late, late in the metropolitan sense rather than the moral one: São Paulo traffic can turn a short ride into an hour of brake lights. By then the plan was already collapsing under his fatigue, and he kept trying to rescue it with what he had left, walking, waiting, eating, and telling himself he was still handling it.

Somewhere past ten, the restaurants one pictures for a birthday in Brazil had closed. Paulista itself was still awake, a Friday crowd filling its sidewalk patios, so the birthday dinner became a meal at one of those, and the owner, with the kindness usually saved for stray animals, let the phone drink from an outlet while they ate. It came back a percent at a time (one, two, three): enough to coordinate, enough to summon a ride, and enough to keep up the increasingly ridiculous impression that he was still in charge.

It's funny what we call "in charge." Having enough battery to summon an Uber doesn't mean you have enough left to be a proper human being.

Around midnight, a ride took them back to Berrini, an hour away, where the district had shut down long before; midnight with the kitchens dark isn't romantic, it's just late. The lobby's brightness felt almost accusatory, as if it had stayed up to witness them: a small republic of electricity in a district that had powered down. The patio's outlet had put a little life back into the phone, but it couldn't recharge the plan that had died inside it.

Princess crossed the lobby quietly, but not calmly: she opened and closed her purse, glanced at nothing in particular, adjusted something that didn't need adjusting. She had spent the day being serious for other people (sessions, schedules, a conference), then sat through São Paulo traffic to arrive at a celebration that was already running down. He had spent the day fighting disorder with a dying battery.

He would later remember almost nothing of the elevator, and little of the hallway beyond carpets pulled up for renovation, the floor showing its bare underlayer. Perhaps a card swipe, two or three tries before the green light. Then the room, and with it something familiar that, he would realize later, wasn't the room at all. (Not a place. A pattern.)

Of what Princess said, he would keep nothing but the tone: a firm sentence, then a sharper one. Sleep deprivation, stress and drink make poor archivists. The words dissolved into his own defensive reading of them, and he never asked her to repeat them.

What he did keep was his own answer. He reached for the cheapest weapon he had, dismissal: a label that reduced her to a nuisance. (Think "Frankly, my dear," minus the eloquence, and minus any excuse.)

The sentence landed, and the air changed.

That man was me.

What frightened me afterward wasn't the argument, or even the birthday gone flat. It was how familiar the sentence felt. I had done this before, in another era and another city, and the grooves had evidently been waiting under the floorboards the whole time. The moment the system got tired enough, they took control again. Years are a long interval for a human heart and an embarrassingly short one for a pattern. Time alone had refactored nothing.

Psychologists call it chronic PTSD; I call it phantom rails. With the "in charge" part of my brain dark and shuttered, I had the reflexes of a haunted reptile, and I couldn't pull myself off the tracks.

I apologized afterward, more than once, and meant it. She said it was all right, told me not to dwell on it and to enjoy the rest of the trip, and she meant that too. But apologies are often what we offer when we have not yet understood what we are really apologizing for. I am still working that out. The word itself was never really the point; the groove that chose it for me was.

We like to believe that time heals and distance resets, that something is in the past because the calendar has marched beyond it. Patterns don't live in calendars, though. They live in architecture, and architecture doesn't change because you've moved the furniture.

In childhood I had a basket, my one tether to a world I thought had been taken from me. In São Paulo I had a phone dying in my hand. The basket held continuity and the phone held coordination, and each became sacred the moment I realized how much of my sanity I had outsourced into it.

Camus went looking for deserts and islands, places to turn away from the world long enough to understand it. My desert that night was a dead battery, and the silence it forced on a mind running on fumes. This volume goes looking in places like that: small crevices, dark corners, the quiet mechanisms that reproduce themselves inside lives and institutions whenever the lights dim and the batteries run low.

When the basket slips, or the battery dies, what, exactly, is still there?
