OVERTURE TO VOLUME II

A Nagging Stillness

> "In order to understand the world, one has to turn away from it on occasion." --- **Albert Camus**

A man crossed a hotel lobby in São Paulo's Berrini business district a little after midnight, beside a woman he called Princess. Outside, the city had switched to dark mode for the night: restaurants closed, the towers blank behind their glass, the sidewalks so empty you could have counted the cracks. Only the hotel stayed stubbornly awake (polished floors, bright ceiling panels, citrus-clean air, and night staff trained in the calm face reserved for late arrivals).

In his hand was a phone that was more corpse than device.

He had spent the day trying to do something simple: celebrate a birthday in a city that didn't speak his language, and not just any birthday, but the kind that quietly insists the evening mean something. He had imagined candles, a real dinner, something local enough to feel true rather than bought from a guidebook. He had prepared the modern way, with a few hopeful pins on a map, half a plan, and a quiet faith that the network would hold and the battery would cooperate.

The battery did not cooperate. It didn't fail dramatically (no flames, no suicidal leap into traffic); it simply declined, steadily and professionally, much like its owner. He had come in on a long flight and two overnight buses, and was running on street food, late hours, and the kind of drinks that don't add courage so much as subtract supervision. He wandered Avenida Paulista on momentum, walking, checking, waiting and rechecking, while the device holding the map of his intentions drifted toward zero.

He had asked Princess to bring his charger when her conference ended. It seemed a small request, a footnote of plastic and wire that shouldn't have the power to decide a night. But in a city where you can't easily improvise in the local language, a phone is your translator, navigator, memory and coordinator, an outsourced cortex. When it dies, your options go with it, and you are left with whatever patterns a tired mind can still run unassisted.

She arrived late, late in the metropolitan sense rather than the moral one: brake lights, distance, and one-hour rides that mature into small political systems of their own. By then the plan was already collapsing under his fatigue, and he kept trying to rescue it with what he had left, walking, waiting, eating, and telling himself he was still handling it.

Somewhere past ten, everything reached zero at once: the clock, the city, the battery, and the man. They drifted onto a roadside patio, one of those aggressively ordinary places that becomes holy the moment you need it not to fail, and the proprietor, with the mercy usually reserved for wounded animals, let the phone drink from an outlet. The future came back in meager percentages (one, two, three), enough to coordinate, enough to summon a ride, and enough to preserve the increasingly ridiculous impression that he was still in charge of the story.

It's funny what we call "in charge." Having enough battery to summon an Uber doesn't mean you have enough left to be a proper human being.

By the time the ride brought them back from Paulista, the city had closed its better chapters. The restaurants one imagines when imagining a birthday in Brazil were shuttered, and midnight with the kitchens dark isn't romantic; it's just late. The lobby's brightness felt almost accusatory, as if it had stayed up specifically to witness them: a small republic of electricity floating in a district that had powered down. His phone was no longer dead, only limping, in sympathy with its owner.

Princess moved through the lobby with the kind of restraint that looks like composure until you notice the small betrayals: the purse opened and closed, a glance at nothing in particular, a needless adjustment of something already in place. She had spent the day being serious for other people (sessions, schedules, professional gravity), then sat through São Paulo traffic to arrive at a celebration already flattening under entropy. He had spent the day fighting disorder with a dying battery.

He would later remember almost nothing of the elevator, and little of the hallway beyond carpets pulled up for renovation, the floor showing its bare underlayer. Perhaps a card swipe, two or three tries before the green light. Then the room, and with it the conviction that they had walked into something familiar that wasn't the room at all. (Not a place. A pattern.)

Of what Princess said, he would keep nothing but the tone: a firm sentence, then a sharper one. Sleep deprivation, stress and drink make poor archivists. The words dissolved into his own defensive reading of them, and he never asked her to repeat them.

What he did keep was his own answer. He reached for the cheapest lever in the human interface, dismissal: a label, one of those small, efficient cruelties that reduce a person to a nuisance-word and call the reduction relief. (Think "Frankly, my dear," minus the eloquence, and minus any excuse.)

The sentence landed, and the air changed.

---

That man was me.

What frightened me afterward wasn't the argument, or even the birthday gone flat. It was how familiar the sentence felt. I had done this before, in another era and another city, and the grooves had evidently been waiting under the floorboards the whole time. The moment the system got tired enough, they took control again, without even the courtesy of surprise. Fifteen years is a long interval for a human heart and an embarrassingly short one for a pattern. Time alone had refactored nothing.

Psychologists call it chronic PTSD; I call them phantom rails. With the "in charge" part of my brain dark and shuttered, I had the reflexes of a haunted reptile, and I couldn't pull myself off the tracks.

I apologized afterward, more than once, and meant it. She said it was all right, told me not to dwell on it and to enjoy the rest of the trip, and she meant that too. But apologies are often what we offer when we have not yet understood what we are really apologizing for. I am still working that out. The word itself was never really the point; the groove that chose it for me was.

We like to believe that time heals and distance resets, that something is in the past because the calendar has marched beyond it. Patterns don't live in calendars, though. They live in architecture, and architecture doesn't change because you've moved the furniture.

In childhood I had a basket, my one tether to a world I thought had been taken from me. In São Paulo I had a phone dying in my hand. The basket held continuity and the phone held coordination, and each became sacred the moment I realized how much of my sanity I had outsourced into it.

Camus went looking for deserts and islands, places to turn away from the world long enough to understand it. My desert that night was a dead battery, and the silence it forced on a mind running on fumes. This volume goes looking in places like that: small crevices, dark corners, the quiet mechanisms that reproduce themselves inside lives and institutions whenever the lights dim and the batteries run low.

When the basket slips, or the battery dies, what, exactly, is still there?
