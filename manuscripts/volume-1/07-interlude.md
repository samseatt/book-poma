INTERLUDE 7

# The E-conomy

*How the Web Turned Code into Capital*

*WEB*

When I finally crossed Lake Washington into Bellevue, I achieved the rarest milestone in capitalism: I stopped being poor without becoming rich. Economists have no word for this, but landlords do: first month's rent plus deposit.

My apartment sat just across 148th Avenue from Redmond, which meant I lived in Bellevue but breathed Microsoft. The corporate campus spread nearby like a geometric labyrinth, low-slung buildings of glass and concrete tucked into the pines, consecrated to the one true deity of the Pacific Northwest. On my jogs, I liked to take a detour through the campus, and I half feared that a gaze from one of those office windows could have me instantly deprecated, for reasons unexplainable to a mortal. But armed with ritual protection of *Ctrl-Alt-Delete*, I somehow persevered.

My job, across the street on the Redmond side, placed me in the extended sprawl of the mothership, working on debugging tools for embedded software, which is what you do when you want to live near the king but not so near that he notices you. Debuggers are the confession booths of computing: they report every sin a program commits, in chronological order. Our tools had grown up on Unix workstations, and we were porting them to Windows as Windows NT arrived, Microsoft's new 32-bit operating system, whose early versions we worked with while the old 16-bit Windows still had to be humored through a translation layer charmingly called "thunking." I had never liked the old Windows. NT was different. Underneath the familiar windows, it finally felt like a real operating system, the closest thing to Unix that Redmond had produced. I forgave the king a little.

My next job pulled me further into the hardware and communication spines of computing, at a telephony company up the coast. The company's even-keeled CEO had grown up in the general orbit of Bill Gates, and the rivalry was clear: every mention of Windows came with a tone normally reserved for mold. Not to be outdone by Gates, he afforded me a spacious window office, all for myself. Like his old friend he also cherished the type of quality that can only come from a deeper contemplation and solid engineering.

It was a true confidence builder. I designed and built systems, finished a project in record time, then led another that came in better than anyone expected, and across the hall I could watch the hardware of the previous generation being built on the assembly floor. But the technology was changing, and so was the town. You could smell it in the air: a mixture of ambition, espresso and the faint electrical crackle of an industry preparing to molt.

## The Web Ate Everything

The web had arrived in Seattle about when I did. In the season of my arrival, a team of students and staff at the University of Illinois released Mosaic for Windows and the Macintosh, the browser that first made the web easy for ordinary people, with pictures sitting right inside the text.[^v1-i7-mosaic] Within months, one of its creators had co-founded Netscape and brought much of the team along; Netscape's Navigator conquered the young web; and Microsoft, waking late, launched Internet Explorer and the first browser war, a conflict whose casualties included standards, sleep cycles and one very confused blinking-text tag.

I was not an early convert. Back at USC, when I'd first heard that the web's pages were written in HTML, a plain-text markup language, I'd dismissed it as a stopgap: hurried, inelegant and wasteful, spelling out in readable text what any self-respecting protocol would encode compactly. Surely something richer would replace it within a few years. Decades later, I'm still waiting. HTML won for the same reason Unix did in the last interlude: worse is better. Anyone could read it, anyone could write it, and any machine could display it, more or less. When JavaScript arrived, a scripting language famously designed in about ten days, it made pages interactive, and made my objections bearable, without ever making them wrong.[^v1-i7-js] The web was never clean. It was simply everywhere.

In the spring of my third Seattle year, I dove in headfirst, joining a web and multimedia company owned by one of the big personal-computer makers. On my first day of orientation, I sheepishly asked a new colleague, "What exactly do you mean when you say 'URL'?" Coming from the pre-web world, it was a sacrilegious question that earned me a sympathetic, amused smile and the advice that I had 'a lot to cover.' Yet, by day three hundred and sixty-five, the industry was treating me like a seasoned sage of software, networking, and everything web. I had ascended from a stray dog scavenging the parking lots of corporate parks and roadside motels to a highly sought-after expert.

A year later, I joined an established software company carrying its customers' old systems onto the web, where every portal was trying to talk to servers that still believed it was 1987.

And I became a translator. Not between people, but between eras. I knew servers. I knew networks. I knew what actually happened when you clicked "Submit" on a web form and stared at the spinning wheel, praying to all known deities. Suddenly I was in demand, not because I was a prodigy of design, but because I knew what lived behind the curtain: the dragons in the database, the gremlins in the session state, and the unpaid technical debt of three decades of computing, all meeting the bright-eyed optimism of the web.


## Anatomy of a Click

My first real education in the web came at the multimedia company, where I learned what happens in the second or so between clicking a link and seeing a page. It's worth knowing, because nearly everything later in this book runs on it.

It starts with an address, a URL such as `https://www.example.com/books/fiction`. The part in the middle is a name, and names mean nothing to the network. So the browser first asks the Domain Name System, the distributed directory that Paul Mockapetris designed at ISI in Interlude 5, for an address serving that name, unless it already has one cached. With the number in hand, it opens or reuses a connection, using TCP to keep the conversation reliable, and, if the address begins with `https`, sets up encryption so no one along the route can read what passes between them.

Then it asks. The browser sends a request in HTTP, the web's simple conversational protocol, which Tim Berners-Lee designed at CERN: in effect, *please send me the page at /books/fiction*.[^v1-i7-http] Somewhere, a web server receives the request, runs whatever program is responsible for that page, which may consult a database for the latest prices and inventory, and assembles a reply. The reply comes back as HTML, the plain-text markup I'd once dismissed, describing the page's structure; then, in further requests, the style sheets that describe how it should look and the scripts that make it respond. The browser reads all of it, lays out the text and images, and paints the screen. A typical page today is assembled from dozens of separate requests, many to different servers around the world, and the browser can begin showing the page while the rest is still arriving.

One feature of that conversation turned out to matter more than any other. HTTP is stateless: every request stands alone; the protocol itself does not remember who you are from one click to the next. A shop must add that memory, because a cart needs to remember what's in it. In 1994, Netscape's browser solved the problem with a small piece of text a website could ask the browser to store and return with later requests to that site: the cookie.[^v1-i7-cookie] It was the size of a raisin, with the memory of an elephant, and it gave the web what it had lacked: a way to recognize you. Carts, logins, recommendations and, eventually, tracking came to rely on it. Often it carried only an identifier; the elephant's memory lived on the server.

## The Web's Toolkit

Underneath every page sit three languages, each with one job. HTML describes structure: this is a heading, this a paragraph, this a link, this a form. CSS, cascading style sheets, describes appearance: fonts, colors, spacing, layout, so that the same structure can look different on a phone and a wall-sized screen. And JavaScript describes behavior: what happens when you click, type or scroll. The browser turns the HTML into a living tree of elements, the Document Object Model, and scripts can reach into that tree and change it while you watch, which is how a page updates without reloading.

Behind the page sits the server side, which in my years was a fast-evolving zoo. The earliest dynamic pages ran small programs through an interface called CGI; then came languages built for the job, like PHP, and Microsoft's Active Server Pages. And then there was Java. Sun released it in 1995 with the promise "write once, run anywhere": programs compiled for a virtual machine that could run on any computer.[^v1-i7-java] Its original showcase, small programs called applets running inside web pages, mostly fizzled. But on the server, Java became the workhorse of corporate web systems, the language in which banks and insurers carried their old mainframe systems onto the web. Most serious sites settled into a three-tier shape that still underlies much of the web: a browser for presentation, an application server for business logic, and a database for memory.

## From Pages to Services

At first, the web served pages to people. Soon it was serving data to programs, and that turned out to matter more. Companies wanted their systems to talk to each other over the web, so they needed a common format for data. XML, a stricter cousin of HTML published in 1998, became that format, and elaborate protocols for "web services" were built on top of it, with thick specifications and a fondness for acronyms.[^v1-i7-xml]

The simpler idea won, as it so often does. In 2000, a doctoral dissertation by one of the HTTP specification's own authors described the architectural style that had made the web itself so successful, and called it REST.[^v1-i7-rest] Its principles are few. Everything is a resource with its own address, a URL. You act on resources with the web's handful of standard verbs: GET to read, POST to submit, PUT to create or replace, DELETE to remove. Each request carries everything needed to understand it, so a server needn't remember the previous conversation to interpret the next request; the same statelessness that once made shopping carts so hard became a virtue, because it lets a service spread across thousands of servers. Paired with JSON, a lightweight data format drawn from JavaScript, REST became the dominant way programs talk to each other. Today, many apps on your phone are thin faces on web APIs built around these conventions, the contracts between strangers of the last interlude, carried over the web.

## Teaching the Web to Sell

To sell, the web needed three more things, and it got them within a few years of my arrival. Payment came with encryption: by 1995, Netscape's SSL protocol, and its little padlock, let a browser authenticate a server and encrypt a card number in transit.[^v1-i7-ssl] Trust came from reputation, the web's answer to Akerlof's lemons on a planetary scale: eBay's ratings and Amazon's customer reviews let strangers' opinions, collected at scale, stand in for knowing the shopkeeper.[^v1-i7-reputation] And being found came from search, first from human librarians at Yahoo, then from Google's insight that a page is important if important pages link to it.[^v1-i7-pagerank] Advertising paid for the rest, sold against every pair of eyes a site could attract.[^v1-i7-banner] Capitalism had always tracked labor. The web began tracking intention.

### The Random Surfer

That sentence about important pages sounds circular, and it is; the circle is what makes it work. But before anything can be ranked, it has to be found. A crawler starts from pages it already knows, fetches each one, pulls out every link and adds the new addresses to its queue, a walk across the web that never quite ends. Each fetched page is then turned inside out. Instead of recording which words a page contains, the engine records, for every word, the list of pages that contain it: an *inverted index*, the trick behind the index at the back of a book. In a simple exact-word search, *used Corolla Bellevue* becomes three lists, intersected, and the answer is ready almost before the question is finished. The hard part is the order. A word can appear on a million pages. Which ten does the person see?

Imagine a surfer with no taste and endless patience. On every page, he picks one of its links at random and follows it. Now and then, bored, he types in a random address and starts over somewhere else. Let him wander long enough and he spends more of his time on some pages than on others: the ones that many paths lead into, especially paths from pages he already frequents. The share of his time a page receives is its PageRank. He's the drunkard of Interlude 5, set loose on a network instead of a grid, and this one never needs to find his way home; all that matters is where he keeps ending up. For any page $p$,

$$
R(p) \;=\; \frac{1-d}{N} \;+\; d \sum_{q \to p} \frac{R(q)}{L(q)},
$$

where $N$ is the number of pages, the sum runs over the pages $q$ that link to $p$, $L(q)$ is the number of links leaving $q$, and $d$, usually set near 0.85, is the chance that the surfer follows a link rather than jumping. Each page divides its rank equally among the pages it links to, so a link from a highly ranked page with few outbound links is worth a great deal, and a link from an obscure page with hundreds is worth almost nothing. The definition refers to itself, but it can be solved the way the surfer would solve it: give every page an equal share, pass the shares along the links, and repeat. In the original experiments, the numbers settled within roughly fifty rounds across hundreds of millions of links. The random jumps matter more than they look. Without them, the surfer could be trapped in a corner of the web with no way out, or circle forever; with them, the wandering has exactly one long-run answer.[^v1-i7-pagerank]

Ranking by links harvested millions of judgments nobody had been asked to make: every author who linked to a page had, in effect, voted for it. Votes attract fraud. Within a few years, operators were building *link farms*, thousands of pages that existed only to link to one another and to a paying client, and the engines answered with more signals and penalties, an arms race much like the spam filter's in the last interlude. They also found a richer source of judgment than links: the searches themselves. Which result did people click, and did they come straight back to try another? Every query became a small experiment, and ranking became a learned function, trained on the behavior of the very people it ranked for.[^v1-i7-ranking]

### Paying for the Click

Advertising found the same lever. A search for *running shoes* says more about what a person wants at that moment than a billboard could learn in a lifetime, so the space beside the results became the most valuable in advertising. It's sold one query at a time, by auction, in the fraction of a second before the page appears. Each advertiser bids what a click is worth to it. But the engine is paid only when someone clicks, so it ranks ads not by bid alone but by bid multiplied by the predicted chance of a click. Suppose one advertiser bids two dollars with a predicted click rate of 1 percent, and another bids one dollar at 3 percent. The second is expected to earn the engine three cents each time it's shown, the first only two, so the second takes the top slot. Nor does it pay its full bid. It pays just enough to keep its place: about sixty-seven cents a click, the price at which its expected value would fall to its rival's. The mechanism rewards ads that people want to click, and it makes the whole market hang on a prediction.[^v1-i7-auction]

That prediction became one of the first learning systems to run at the scale of a planet. Every ad shown is an example, and every click or non-click its label. Billions of them a day, described by the query, the ad's words, the hour and the device, train a model that has to stay current as tastes, seasons and fashions shift, and a sliver of extra accuracy is worth a fortune.[^v1-i7-ctr] Interlude 6's learned score had found its market: the model supplies a probability, and the auction turns it into a price.

## What a Dot-Com Was

Practically, a dot-com was a company whose business lived mainly on the web, and in the late 1990s it was also a theory of the future. On the web, delivering one more copy of information can cost almost nothing (the grocery van still needs a driver), and many web businesses grow more valuable the more people use them (more sellers attract more buyers, who attract more sellers), so the leader can pull ever further ahead: Brian Arthur's increasing returns, at the speed of light. Hence the creed: get big fast, measure success in "eyeballs," raise money from venture capitalists, and worry about profit later. When Netscape went public in 1995 without ever having made a profit, its shares more than doubled on the first day, and for the next four years money poured into anything with ".com" in its name.[^v1-i7-netscape]

Philosophically, the dot-com era was three of the last chapter's ideas colliding. It was Coase's question reopened: if the web made some coordination much cheaper, the boundaries of firms could move, and whole industries could be rebuilt around the network. It was Schumpeter's creative destruction playing out in real time against bookstores, travel agents and newspapers.[^v1-i7-schumpeter] And it was Chapter 7's reflexive loop in its purest form: companies valued on stories about future traffic, by investors buying because other investors were buying. <!-- AI draft, 2026-11-14. -->Chapter 7's arithmetic shows why the swings were so large: when a company's expected growth sits close to the rate at which the future is discounted, a point of belief can double or quarter its price.<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-14. --> The bubble was real and foolish. And the transformation it was overpricing was real too. Most of the companies died. The web they'd been betting on did not.

## Web 1.0, 2.0 and 3.0

The web's eras are usually numbered, a little after the fact. What I worked on in the 1990s came to be called Web 1.0: mostly static pages, published by a few and read by many, with commerce bolted on. Around 2004, a technology publisher's conference popularized "Web 2.0" for what came next: a web where users wrote as much as they read.[^v1-i7-web2] Blogs, wikis, photo sharing, video and, above all, social networks turned readers into authors, and the platforms that hosted them grew enormous on content their users created for free. Technically, Web 2.0 gained much of its responsiveness from pages that could fetch data in the background and update themselves, a technique christened AJAX in 2005, and by open APIs that let services be mixed and remixed. Economically, it was the platform model: whoever owns the place where everyone gathers owns the most valuable thing on the web.

"Web 3.0" has meant two different things, which is worth knowing because people argue past each other about it. For Tim Berners-Lee, it meant the Semantic Web: pages that carry machine-readable meaning, so that software could understand what a page is about rather than merely displaying it.[^v1-i7-semantic] Later, "Web3" came to mean a web built on blockchains, where ownership of data, identity and money would be recorded in shared ledgers instead of on platforms' servers, an idea Volume II will examine when it turns to consensus and digital ownership.[^v1-i7-web3] Neither has fully arrived. But a third candidate has appeared, uninvited by either camp: a web whose most active readers are no longer people at all, but AI agents, reading, summarizing and acting on pages on their users' behalf. Berners-Lee's dream of a web machines could understand is being realized, though not in the way anyone planned.

## The Web's Unpaid Teachers

The web also needed to tell its people from its programs. Bots signed up for free email accounts by the million to send spam from them, and swept up concert tickets faster than any fan could click. The answer, around 2000, turned Turing's test around: instead of a person judging whether a machine could pass for human, a machine judged whether you could. The name says as much, a CAPTCHA being a *Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart*. It showed a word warped and smeared until the character-recognition programs of the day couldn't read it, though most people still could.

Then the waste became visible. Something like two hundred million of these little puzzles were being solved every day, each a few seconds of human perception thrown away. reCAPTCHA put them to work. It showed two words: one the system already knew, as a check, and one from a scanned page of an old book or newspaper that the character-recognition software had failed to read. Get the known word right, and your reading of the unknown one counted as a vote; once enough readers agreed, the word was settled. People were proofreading a library a few seconds at a time, often without noticing.[^v1-i7-captcha] Later the puzzles changed: house numbers photographed from the street, then grids of pictures (*select every square with a traffic light*), whose answers, its owner said, also helped annotate images for machine learning. Elsewhere, the largest labeled image collection of its day, ImageNet, was sorted into thousands of categories by workers hired piece by piece through a web marketplace, and it was the benchmark on which Interlude 3's AlexNet won.[^v1-i7-imagenet]

The irony completed itself. The warped words that had once separated people from programs were eventually read by neural networks with near-perfect accuracy, better than many people managed.[^v1-i7-solved] So the test moved out of sight. Many sites now assess signals from a visitor's interaction with them, and a model returns a score for how human the session looks, which the site turns into let through, challenge or block: the score and thresholds of the last interlude's fraud check, aimed at us. Web 2.0 had made its users unpaid authors. The CAPTCHA made them unpaid teachers.

## The Commons Meets Commerce

While the money poured in, the man who had kept the internet's books for nearly thirty years made his last stand. Jon Postel had spent his career at ISI administering the internet's names and numbers as a public trust, on behalf of a community that had always governed itself by rough consensus. Now those names had become valuable property. Domain names were being sold, a single government contractor controlled the master list, and Washington was drafting plans for the internet's future governance.

On January 28, 1998, Postel asked the operators of eight root-server operators, responsible for servers at the very top of the Domain Name System's tree, to take their master data from his computer at ISI instead of from the contractor's. They did. For several days, those servers quietly took their root-zone updates from one engineer in Marina del Rey. The data itself did not change. He called it a test. Officials in Washington called it something closer to a hijacking, and within a week the change was reversed.[^v1-i7-postel] But the point had been made, whether he intended it or not: the internet's deepest authority still rested, in practice, on the trust of the engineers who ran it. That fall, a new nonprofit, ICANN, was created to take over the stewardship Postel had carried, with a governing model meant to include many stakeholders rather than one government.

He didn't live to see it work. In October 1998, Jon Postel died of complications following heart surgery, at fifty-five. His friend Vint Cerf, one of the internet's founders, wrote a farewell published as one of the very Requests for Comments Postel had edited for decades.[^v1-i7-cerf] It's tempting to say that his heart, which had carried so much of the network, couldn't carry that last year. It would be sentimental to claim it, and I don't. But the internet lost its quiet steward in the very year its commons was being turned into a market, and the timing has always felt like more than coincidence to me.

## The Commons Becomes Training Material

The web's early builders made their commons with small courtesies. In 1994 they settled on a simple convention: a website could place a file called `robots.txt` at its root, telling automated crawlers which pages they were welcome to visit and which to leave alone.[^v1-i7-robots] The file could ask a crawler to keep out; it could not lock the door. It relied on the crawler keeping the agreement. <!-- AI draft, 2026-11-17. -->In the last interlude's terms, it was a promise kept outside the procedure, with nothing but manners to hold it there.<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-17. --> Major search engines adopted it, and the web's search engines grew up inside that etiquette, taking pages to index them and sending readers back in return.

Then the web acquired a new kind of reader. Large language models, the subject of Interlude 11, learn by reading enormous amounts of human writing, and the largest source of human writing ever assembled is the web. A nonprofit called Common Crawl has archived much of the public web since 2008, many billions of pages, and archives like it supplied a large share of the text on which today's models were trained.[^v1-i7-crawl] Forum posts written to help strangers, product reviews, personal homepages and arguments in comment threads became part of the education of machines, often without their authors participating in that decision. <!-- AI draft, 2026-11-17. -->Other learning systems drew on the commons' quieter layers: the links that ranked its pages, the clicks that tuned the rankings, and the warped words and traffic lights that taught machines to read and see.<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-17. -->

That changes the bargain the commons was built on. A search engine took a page and sent readers back; a model takes the page and may answer the reader's question itself, so that the reader never visits. Many publishers have since blocked AI crawlers in their `robots.txt` files, and the old courtesy is straining under a use its authors never imagined.[^v1-i7-optout] There is a question beneath those disputes. The web became an immense act of shared authorship, and it has become, in effect, the memory from which a new kind of mind is being built. Chapter 7 found money to be a society's memory, compressed into a single number. The web is a richer memory, of what people knew, argued, feared and joked about, and it's being compressed again, this time into the weights of a model.

## Bob, Through the Looking-Glass: The Commons That Became a Mind

*The twist, in one line: if a commons can be compressed into a learning system, perhaps the obligations to keep that commons alive can travel with what the system learns, even when its individual sources no longer can.*

In *Through the Looking-Glass*, the White Queen offers Alice work with jam every other day. The difficulty is that today never qualifies. The benefit is always safely yesterday's or tomorrow's.[^v1-i7-lookingglass] A living commons cannot subsist on that employment contract. The people making its next useful observation need to eat today.

Ownership is one question. Continuation is another. Suppose a model becomes good enough to answer questions from a community's accumulated work. The answers may spare its readers a great deal of effort while removing the visits, income or new participants that kept the community producing answers in the first place. It can inherit a library and help close the doors. What would make the inheritance carry a duty to keep the library living?

Chapter 7's dollar loses the autobiography of a trade while retaining its amount. A model does a more complicated compression: many contributions alter the same parameters, and one contribution can alter many. A useful answer need not have a single sentence we can point to and call its parent. Demanding an exact ancestry for every answer may be asking the compressed thing to supply the very detail its usefulness required it to discard. Attribution methods can help; they do not turn every answer into an itemized invoice.[^v1-i7-obligations]

I would try keeping a second kind of memory beside the learned one: a memory of obligations. Training could condense what a community knows without being allowed to condense away the terms of participation. A model and its descendants could carry a shared, inspectable account of the commons they draw from and the commitments attached to that use. Fine-tuning would add to the account; teaching a smaller model from a larger one would not automatically wipe it clean. The last interlude asked where promises live when procedures thaw. Here even the procedure's knowledge has been melted down.

Those commitments need not all be royalties. A model serving a medical reference community might help fund its next evidence review, return discovered errors, or perform work the community chooses. A local-language archive might need transcription, storage and new speakers rather than a cheque split into microscopic pieces. The unit of return could be the activity that renews the knowledge, not a guessed price for each sentence. The participants would have to help set those terms; otherwise we have simply appointed the model's owner curator of everybody else's needs.

The wager is whether we can make learning inherit a relationship, rather than merely acquire material. Can a system become better at answering from a commons while also making that commons more capable of producing what it does not yet know? That would turn some of its apparent debt into a continuing dependence worth tending. We could test it in a small, consenting community: compare a system that only cites its sources with one whose use supplies agreed resources or work back to them, then watch whether new knowledge, correction and participation actually survive. A flattering acknowledgement is not the same outcome.

There are awkward edges worth leaving sharp. Who represents a dispersed community? How does an obligation follow a distilled model whose maker refuses to declare its parent? A registry cannot prevent someone copying weights and abandoning the account. Nor should a claim of ancestry become a hereditary toll on every future thought. The terms need limits, challenge and revision, and the people making new knowledge need a way out. These are part of the research, not administrative details to be supplied once the lovely idea has shipped.

Volume III will try to design for those questions. For now, it's enough to notice the shape of the story: the web began as a commons, became a marketplace, and is becoming a mind.

## The Tragedy of My Dot Commons

Seattle had become my commons. It was where the fraternity's prophecy had half come true; where I had arrived to sleep in the Corolla and ended up beside a marina; where a man who once asked what a URL was had found his way into the heart of the web. By the end of the decade, I had made it all the way to the dot-com: a day job, a title at a startup, and a venture of my own, woven together with partners on three continents, every piece finally pulling in the same direction.

One day I was writing scripts for machines that didn't care where I lived; the next I was packing boxes for a border that very much did.

I crossed another border, and the structure I'd built came apart behind me, the way a commons does when the people tending it scatter. What it taught me about agreement, and about how easily it can fall short of consensus, is a story for a later interlude. The next chapter is about the border.

Earlier graphical browsers existed; Mosaic's inline images and easy installation made it the first to reach a mass audience. Andreessen and Jim Clark founded Netscape in April 1994, recruiting several members of the Mosaic team; Microsoft released Internet Explorer in August 1995. [Contemporary federal computing-program account](https://www.nitrd.gov/pubs/1996supplement/NITRD_Supplement-1996.pdf). The November 1993 cross-platform availability is separate from Mosaic’s earlier Unix release.

[^v1-i7-js]: Brendan Eich created JavaScript at Netscape in about ten days in May 1995. On "worse is better," see Interlude 6. [Eich’s own recollection](https://brendaneich.com/2011/06/new-javascript-engine-module-owner/). The ten days refer to the first engine prototype, not all subsequent language development.

[^v1-i7-http]: Tim Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web at CERN in March 1989 and built the first web server, browser and HTML/HTTP implementation in 1990. CERN released the web's software into the public domain in April 1993. [CERN history](https://web30.web.cern.ch/web-history.html). The example describes the familiar TCP-based HTTP stack. HTTP/3 instead runs over QUIC; see [RFC 9114](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9114.html).

[^v1-i7-cookie]: Lou Montulli implemented cookies in Netscape Navigator in 1994; they were later standardized in RFC 2109 (1997). [Original specification](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2109.html). A cookie often identifies server-side session state; it need not contain the cart itself.

[^v1-i7-java]: Sun Microsystems released Java publicly in May 1995. [Oracle’s Java tutorial history](https://docs.oracle.com/cd/E19798-01/821-1770/gcrkp/index.html).

[^v1-i7-xml]: [XML 1.0, W3C Recommendation, 10 February 1998](https://www.w3.org/TR/1998/REC-xml-19980210); [SOAP 1.1, W3C Note, 8 May 2000](https://www.w3.org/TR/2000/NOTE-SOAP-20000508/). A W3C Note is not the same publication status as a Recommendation.

[^v1-i7-rest]: Roy Thomas Fielding, [*Architectural Styles and the Design of Network-based Software Architectures* (2000), chapter 5](https://ics.uci.edu/~fielding/pubs/dissertation/rest_arch_style.htm), particularly §5.1.3. Statelessness concerns request/session context, not the absence of server-held resources. HTTP method semantics are in [RFC 9110, §§9.3.3–9.3.4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html): POST submits content for processing; PUT creates or replaces the target representation. JSON was popularized by Douglas Crockford and documented in informational [RFC 4627 (2006)](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4627.html).

[^v1-i7-ssl]: Netscape released SSL 2.0 in 1995; its successor, TLS, secures the web today. [IETF’s historical/deprecation account](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6176.html). SSL 2.0 is obsolete and insecure; the historical passage is not advice to deploy it.

[^v1-i7-reputation]: Pierre Omidyar, [“Founder’s Letter,” 26 February 1996](https://pages.ebay.com/services/forum/feedback-foundersnote.html), introduces AuctionWeb’s Feedback Forum. Amazon dates its customer reviews to its 1995 launch in [its own retrospective](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/amazon-ai/amazon-improves-customer-reviews-with-generative-ai).

[^v1-i7-pagerank]: Sergey Brin and Lawrence Page, "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine" (1998), *Computer Networks and ISDN Systems* 30: 107–117, §2.1, which describes the "random surfer" and a damping factor usually set to 0.85; Lawrence Page, Sergey Brin, Rajeev Motwani and Terry Winograd, "The PageRank Citation Ranking: Bringing Order to the Web," Stanford InfoLab technical report (1999), which reports convergence in roughly fifty iterations on a graph of about 322 million links. Google was incorporated in September 1998. The 1998 paper writes the jump term as $(1-d)$; dividing by $N$, as here, makes the ranks a probability distribution summing to one. Pages without outbound links are treated as jumping anywhere. With the jump included, the surfer's walk is a Markov chain with a unique stationary distribution, computable by power iteration. The figure's percentages are computed from the formula for its six-page graph. Search ranking has long combined PageRank with many other signals. [1998 paper](https://research.google/pubs/the-anatomy-of-a-large-scale-hypertextual-web-search-engine/). For the figure, A links to B; B to A; C to A and F; D to A; E to A and F; F to E. At damping 0.85 their stationary shares are 41.408, 37.697, 2.5, 2.5, 8.655 and 7.241 percent. Circle sizes are illustrative; percentages carry the numerical result. [1999 report, convergence discussion and figure 4](https://storm.cis.fordham.edu/~gweiss/selected-papers/classic-pagerank-paper.pdf). The reported 52 iterations are an experiment, not a general bound on power-iteration convergence.

[^v1-i7-banner]: The first widely recognized banner ad, for AT&T, appeared on HotWired in October 1994. [Interview with its creator, Joe McCambley (2014)](https://digiday.com/media/banner-ad-creator/). Several advertisers launched banners with HotWired; the wording does not assert an uncontested invention priority.

[^v1-i7-ranking]: On link spam, Zoltán Gyöngyi and Hector Garcia-Molina, "Web Spam Taxonomy," *Proceedings of the First International Workshop on Adversarial Information Retrieval on the Web* (AIRWeb 2005). On learning to rank from clicks, Thorsten Joachims, "Optimizing Search Engines Using Clickthrough Data," *Proceedings of the 8th ACM SIGKDD Conference* (2002): 133–142; Chris Burges et al., "Learning to Rank Using Gradient Descent," *Proceedings of ICML* (2005). Clicks are biased evidence: people favor top positions regardless of quality, and engines must correct for that bias. [Joachims’s 2002 paper](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~tj/publications/joachims_02c.pdf).

[^v1-i7-auction]: Benjamin Edelman, Michael Ostrovsky and Michael Schwarz, "Internet Advertising and the Generalized Second-Price Auction: Selling Billions of Dollars Worth of Keywords," *American Economic Review* 97(1) (2007): 242–259; Hal R. Varian, "Position Auctions," *International Journal of Industrial Organization* 25(6) (2007): 1163–1178. The example is simplified: the winner pays $\$2 \times 0.01 / 0.03 \approx \$0.67$, the lowest price at which its bid-times-click-rate still matches the runner-up's. Real systems weight bids by broader quality scores, add reserve prices and small increments, and have changed auction rules over time. The generalized second-price auction does not in general make truthful bidding optimal. [Original auction analysis](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/abs/10.1257/aer.97.1.242).

[^v1-i7-ctr]: H. Brendan McMahan et al., "Ad Click Prediction: A View from the Trenches," *Proceedings of the 19th ACM SIGKDD Conference* (2013): 1222–1230, doi:10.1145/2487575.2488200; Xinran He et al., "Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook," *Proceedings of ADKDD* (2014). "One of the first" is a characterization of scale and continuous retraining, not a priority claim. [Google authors’ 2013 paper](https://research.google/pubs/ad-click-prediction-a-view-from-the-trenches/).

[^v1-i7-netscape]: Netscape's initial public offering on 9 August 1995 was priced at $28 a share; the stock opened at $71 and closed at about $58. [Contemporary AP report, 10 August 1995](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/VA-news/VA-Pilot/issues/1995/vp950810/08100449.htm).

[^v1-i7-schumpeter]: Joseph A. Schumpeter, *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942).

[^v1-i7-web2]: Tim O'Reilly, "What Is Web 2.0" (2005); the term was popularized by the Web 2.0 Conference of 2004. Jesse James Garrett coined "Ajax" in "Ajax: A New Approach to Web Applications" (February 2005). [O’Reilly’s essay](https://www.oreilly.com/pub/a/web2/archive/what-is-web-20.html).

[^v1-i7-semantic]: Tim Berners-Lee, James Hendler and Ora Lassila, "The Semantic Web" (2001), *Scientific American* 284(5): 34–43. [W3C’s Semantic Web bibliography](https://www.w3.org/wiki/SemanticWebFaq).

[^v1-i7-captcha]: Luis von Ahn, Manuel Blum, Nicholas J. Hopper and John Langford, “CAPTCHA: Using Hard AI Problems for Security,” *EUROCRYPT 2003*: 294–311; Luis von Ahn et al., [“reCAPTCHA: Human-Based Character Recognition via Web Security Measures,” *Science* 321 (2008): 1465–1468](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~biglou/reCAPTCHA_Science.pdf). The paper estimates more than 100 million daily CAPTCHAs and explains control words and repeated votes. The roughly 200 million estimate used here is reported in the [NSF’s project account](https://www.nsf.gov/impacts/captcha). Google acquired reCAPTCHA in 2009. Turing’s imitation game appears in “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” *Mind* 59 (1950): 433–460.

[^v1-i7-imagenet]: Jia Deng et al., "ImageNet: A Large-Scale Hierarchical Image Database," *Proceedings of IEEE CVPR* (2009): 248–255, on labeling through Amazon Mechanical Turk; Olga Russakovsky et al., "ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge," *International Journal of Computer Vision* 115 (2015): 211–252. AlexNet won the 2012 challenge; see Interlude 3. [Authors’ 2009 presentation](https://www.image-net.org/static_files/papers/ImageNet_VSS2009.pdf).

[^v1-i7-solved]: Ian J. Goodfellow et al., "Multi-digit Number Recognition from Street View Imagery Using Deep Convolutional Neural Networks," *ICLR* (2014), reporting 99.8 percent accuracy on the hardest category of reCAPTCHA's distorted text. For human performance, Elie Bursztein et al., "How Good Are Humans at Solving CAPTCHAs? A Large Scale Evaluation," *IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy* (2010). Google's reCAPTCHA v3 (2018) returns a score from 0.0 to 1.0 and leaves the response to the site; score-based checks are only one family of current bot defenses. [Goodfellow and colleagues’ paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6082). The 99.8 percent result is task-specific, not a claim that all bot defenses were solved. The score/action interface is described in [Google’s reCAPTCHA v3 documentation](https://developers.google.com/recaptcha/docs/v3).

[^v1-i7-postel]: The Internet Society’s [IANA transition timeline](https://www.internetsociety.org/ianatimeline/) dates Postel’s test to 28 January 1998. Eight operators redirected their root-zone update source to his server; the root-zone data itself was unchanged. See also Milton L. Mueller, *Ruling the Root* (2002). This did not send users’ ordinary queries through Postel’s computer. ICANN was incorporated in September 1998.

[^v1-i7-cerf]: Jon Postel died on 16 October 1998. Vint Cerf, "I Remember IANA," RFC 2468 (October 1998). [Cerf’s memorial](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2468.html).

[^v1-i7-robots]: Martijn Koster proposed the Robots Exclusion Protocol in 1994; it was formalized as RFC 9309 in 2022. [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309.html). Section 1 explicitly distinguishes requested crawler behavior from access authorization.

[^v1-i7-crawl]: Common Crawl, founded in 2007, has published regular web archives since 2008. Filtered versions of its data made up the majority of the training text for models such as GPT-3; see Tom B. Brown et al., "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (2020), arXiv:2005.14165. [GPT-3 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165). Table 2.2 assigns filtered Common Crawl 60 percent of sampled training tokens. [Common Crawl](https://www.commoncrawl.org/about) dates its founding to 2007 and web collection to 2008.

[^v1-i7-optout]: Shayne Longpre et al., [“Consent in Crisis: The Rapid Decline of the AI Data Commons” (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14933), tracks changing robots.txt and terms-of-service restrictions across web domains during 2023–24. These signals of permission and refusal do not themselves resolve the legal status of a particular training use.

[^v1-i7-lookingglass]: Lewis Carroll, [*Through the Looking-Glass* (1871), chapter V, “Wool and Water”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12/pg12-images.html). The White Queen’s jam bargain is Carroll’s; its application to the renewal of knowledge commons is the author’s.

[^v1-i7-obligations]: For an example of approximate training-data attribution, Pang Wei Koh and Percy Liang, [“Understanding Black-box Predictions via Influence Functions,” *ICML* (2017): 1885–1894](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/koh17a.html). Attribution is not exact ownership accounting. Inheritable obligations to maintain a knowledge commons, including through distillation, are a design conjecture here; neither this paper nor a registry establishes that they will be complete, enforceable or fair.
