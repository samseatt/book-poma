> *Wo aber Gefahr ist, wächst / Das Rettende auch.*
> *(Yet where danger is, the saving power also grows.)*
> --- Hölderlin, *Patmos*


A loud, groaning plane (a Fokker, most likely) cut its strange path through the sky, its fuselage shuddering and complaining the whole way. Inside, a small boy sat rigid, grinding against an unrest as vast as the sky outside and just as invisible. He was two and a half years old. The world he knew was gone.

Beside him sat a man and a woman, younger than the two people he called his parents. They spoke softly and kindly and tried to calm him, and it made no difference. They were strangers, imposters who seemed to think they had some claim on him.

Home had been torn away: the house, its sounds, the faces he trusted, the persistence that had been his whole small world. What was left was a rattling chamber hurtling through nothing, and his fear.

One thing still tied him to what was real: his basket. A deep, rectangular, woven basket, unremarkable to anyone else, holding his things. To him it was the Ark of the Covenant, the last proof that the old world hadn't been entirely erased.

He asked for it, then demanded it. Was it still there? Had they taken that too?

The man got up, took the boy's hand and walked him down the narrow aisle toward the front of the cabin. And there it was, stowed where the boy couldn't see it from his seat.

Maybe the boy touched it. Maybe he only looked. Either way, for a moment, the terror let go.

The basket was still there.

That boy was me.

The man and the woman were my parents. They had left me for a year in the care of my grandparents while they crossed an ocean, and now they had come back for me. I didn't know that. To me, my grandparents were my parents, and these two were strangers taking me away from them.

The memory didn't travel with me in a straight line. It surfaced years later, out of the blue, when I was an older child, and it has been the first thing I remember ever since: my experiential Big Bang, the first recorded spark of whatever became my conscious self. The innermost Matryoshka doll. A child suspended in a plane, the plane suspended in air, the planet suspended in space, the galaxy in... well, turtles all the way down.

It was also my first meeting with a monster that lives in reality itself: displacement, the dread of not knowing where home has gone, and the discovery of how thin the familiar really is.

Many crossings followed. Each move peeled off another mask and turned up another monster (in systems, in the ground under our feet, in the stars, in people), and with each one the same question came back: is the basket still there? Is anything I knew still real?

This book is the story of those crossings, and of what became of the basket.
