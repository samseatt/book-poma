CHAPTER 1

# The First Horizon

*The Monster of Existence*

*PHYSICS*

> "The cosmos is within us. We are made of star-stuff. We are a way for the universe to know itself." --- **Carl Sagan**[^v1-c1-sagan]

That bubble of Fokker memory, lodged somewhere in my hippocampus and amygdala alike, ended as abruptly as it began: like a quantum fluctuation, brief, unexplainable, and gone before anyone could take a measurement. Its context I reconstructed only later, from the particle tracks of my mother's storytelling. But the physics stayed, or so I was promised. The plane (or perhaps several parallel planes, depending on which universe you ask) landed. My oblivious toddler self was tagged, transported, washed, rinsed, repeated.

The next clear frame I remember: I was happily walking and talking around a big house, fully fluent in the idea that these were my parents, the only ones I'd ever had. The world, I thought, had always been this way. Solid. Predictable. Like a classical fable masquerading as a life, Newtonian in its order, and just as uninterested in my confusion as I was in its origin story.

But the feeling that something was fundamentally off stayed with me, like a loose screw in the fuselage of reality. I did not yet have a word for it. Still, I occasionally wondered: do others know what I do not? Is this world a magic trick, with everyone else in on the secret (my parents, the cook, the people in the shops), and I the only moron who sees nothing but shadows?

The thought came back again and again: perhaps I was the only one left out of the plot. Everyone else could see the wires that made the world dance, while I marveled at the puppets. I hadn't yet heard of Plato's cave (at four, they had me at Play-Doh), but I was living in it: sure there were shadows, unsure I would ever see the light behind them.

## The Monster of Existence

I did not know it then, but I was beginning to sense a preschooler's version of what I'll call the monster of existence: the unease underneath all experience, the question waiting at the threshold of every thought. Why is there something rather than nothing?

Philosophers have called this unease *the Nothing*, *das Nichts*, *the absurd*. Usually it's life's answers that ruin your day; this is a question that threatens the whole week.

Reality felt so unreal to our early ancestors that they replaced it with fiction and then promoted the fiction to truth. We drafted creation stories for everything from gods to empires to economies, from the founding of Rome to the minting of Bitcoin. We installed myths like emergency door plugs, sometimes with the bolts missing. Nothing to worry about: just a neat sleight of hand we still go along with, for lack of better instructions (and lack of refunds). Yet even after we commissioned these handiworks of Romulus and Nakamoto, after all the telescopes and particle accelerators, reality still remains, in many ways, unreal.

Sagan said we are a way for the universe to know itself. It's a generous job description, but we are neither the only way nor the first. In a more modest sense, the universe "knows" itself much earlier and much more quietly: interactions can leave one system carrying a trace of another, long before any nervous system shows up to write about it.[^v1-c1-registration] (Chapter Φ called this registration, and was careful not to call it experience.) And what does "we" even mean, when we try to speak from inside pure physics?

Physics walks with us until it can't. We follow the equations until the trail dissolves into mist, and at that edge (what I would have called, as a child, the sandbox wall) we find ourselves staring at an origin story we can't yet write. Stories step in here, not because we are weak, but because the universe forgot to include a user manual in the box. This book is partly my attempt at such a story: not as doctrine, but as resonance, contouring around the blind spots of physics rather than replacing it.

## The View from Inside

It would be very tempting, and very easy, to start a chapter about physics with the story of the Big Bang: a hot, dense early universe; perhaps an earlier burst of inflation; the electroweak symmetry breaking as the Higgs field settled; quarks locking themselves into protons and neutrons; the first light nuclei forged within minutes; light set free after some 380,000 years; stars kindled.[^v1-c1-timeline] The Open before this chapter told a version of it, in the cadence of Genesis.

But told like that, the story quietly places us outside, watching it happen. No such observation deck is available to us. We are the Big Bang, or at least a late and talkative part of it. Every telescope and every detector we own looks at the universe from the inside, and Chapter Φ already showed the awkwardness of that position: the instruments, the investigator and the thing investigated belong to the same unfolding story.

So what can be said about the outside, if anything? Perhaps only this: whatever it was, it must at least have been possible. Φ's ladder of nothings ended there, on the one rung that can't be removed, because even nothing has to be possible in order to be the case.

I'll tell this chapter the way I encountered it. First, the thought that made the most sense to me when I tried to look from nowhere; then what physics, looking from the only place it can, has to say about it. (Einstein didn't find relativity by solving Newton's equations more carefully. Minkowski later gave special relativity its four-dimensional geometry; Grossmann helped Einstein find the curved geometry needed for general relativity.[^v1-c1-einstein] Sometimes the view has to change before the answer can.)

## The One Free Lunch

Start at the bottom of Φ's ladder. Take away the furniture, the air, the fields, space and time, the laws themselves. What remains? Even nothing would need to be possible in order to be the case. The old maxim puts it neatly: *ab esse ad posse*, from being to possibility.[^v1-c1-abesse] We are here; therefore being here is possible. That much is modest. My next step isn't: I take possibility as the starting condition, rather than another object needing manufacture. Try to deny it while making a possible statement and the floor becomes slippery. Why there should be even that much being-ness is beyond my purview (and, I suspect, everyone's). But there is no other floor on offer.

I don't mean what's physically possible, given our laws. I mean what's possibly possible: every consistent arrangement, every relation among arrangements, every arrangement of relations, without limit. Call it possibility space. Nothing in it costs anything. No contractor delivered it and nobody poured it.

<!-- Author's original passage restored, lightly cleaned, 2026-11-12b. -->
It's not hard to imagine imagining. Our brains do it for a living. One could argue possibility is just a mental construct: that without a brain to spin it, "could" and "might" don't mean anything. But brains don't just hallucinate; they generate models that line up suspiciously well with whatever we call the external world. For a lump of wet tissue, that is an ambitious hobby. (And physics itself can't be written without "would": every law says what would happen in cases that never do.)

When a brain dies, its private reality ceases, but the world it modeled carries on. The same seems true of its imagined possibilities: they don't vanish from whatever "meta-space" we're using just because the little engine that dreamt them has stalled. Possibility, at least conceptually, is freer and more pervasive than matter. Even vacuum energy isn't free; it's just cheap. But possibility? That might be the only true free lunch. Yum.
<!-- End restored passage. -->

Physics offers a suggestive piece of bookkeeping. In a Newtonian model, picture a galaxy riding the expansion at the edge of a uniform ball of matter. Per unit mass, its energy of motion can cancel its gravitational debt to the matter inside the ball:

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{GM}{r} \;=\; 0 ,
$$

put in the expansion law $v = Hr$ and the mass of the ball, and out comes one particular density:

$$
\rho_c \;=\; \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}
$$

Today that is the mass-equivalent of about five hydrogen atoms per cubic meter, counting all forms of cosmic energy, not five actual atoms floating there. In general relativity, this critical density marks zero spatial curvature; combined observations put ours within a fraction of a percent of flat.[^v1-c1-ledger] Flatness does not, by itself, give an expanding universe a well-defined total energy of zero. But the cancellation inspired an audacious prospect: a universe for no money down. What such proposals couldn't get for free was the menu: the laws, the fields, the rule for blowing bubbles. A multiverse inherits that question at a grander scale. Possibility space asks for less. It places nothing anywhere.

Then who lays down the energy to make one arrangement actual? Nobody had to bring in bulldozers: that is the conjecture. "Actual," as the philosopher David Lewis argued, works like "here": it means the world we are in.[^v1-c1-lewis] Once you are yourself a knot of possibilities, tangled up with everything around you, those relations are as solid as anything gets. A programmer would call it a search space. The data sits at some address (or at many), and all we know for certain is that we are at one of them.

But everything possible is a great deal, and a theory that says "everything" has predicted nothing until some possibilities count for more than others. Here I have a place to stand that others built. Max Tegmark noticed that the whole can hold less information than a particular part.[^v1-c1-zeroinfo] A library of every finite book needs only an alphabet and an instruction to enumerate the strings. To hand you an arbitrary book from it, I may need a call number about as long as the book; a highly patterned book gets a discount. Russell Standish turns this into an argument for Occam's razor: observers should expect a simply describable world compatible with their existence.[^v1-c1-standish] <!-- AI draft, 2026-11-14 (review of Codex pass). -->The intuition is a head count. A short recipe leaves nearly everything unsaid, and every way of filling in the unsaid is one more possibility with the same world inside it.<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-14. --> Fix a language of self-delimiting recipes, so one complete recipe cannot be the beginning of another. Then each additional yes-or-no halves that recipe's weight. For a finite record $x$, a universal decoder $U$ adds the weights of every recipe $p$ that produces it:

$$
m_U(x) \;=\; \sum_{p:\,U(p)=x} 2^{-|p|} \;\asymp\; 2^{-K_U(x)}
$$

Here $K_U(x)$ is the length of the shortest recipe, and $\asymp$ means agreement up to fixed multiplicative factors. The relation is a mathematical result within this counting scheme; choosing the scheme as a measure over possible realities is the wager. Occam's razor, on this reading, is no rule of taste for tidy theorists. It's a census—with its counting office declared. (Bayesian inference has an *Occam factor*, too, but that compares models by how well they predict the evidence.)[^v1-c1-occam]

The census matters for the *Boltzmann brain*: in some cosmological models, a lone brain fluctuating into existence with false memories can outnumber observers produced by a long cosmic history.[^v1-c1-bbrain] But "likely" depends on how you count, and there are two ledgers. Inside one given universe, a fluctuation producing a ready-made brain can cost far less entropy than one producing an entire ordered cosmos. That ledger produces the paradox (and a theory which concludes that its own author is probably a hallucination has sawed off the branch it sits on). Across possibility space I want to count recipes. A lawful history can supply a brain's regularities wholesale: laws that fit on a coffee mug, a starting condition, and whatever further information picks out this history and this head. But a fluctuated copy gets the same right to compression. If the two heads are identical, their shortest descriptions are identical too; I cannot charge one by the synapse and give the other a loyalty card.

The wager therefore concerns the world that continues around the head. Can a short history keep supplying coherent experience more cheaply than a succession of unrelated accidents? Markus Müller's observer-state framework gives that question a precise algorithmic treatment, under explicit assumptions.[^v1-c1-address] It does not make an actual cosmological population of Boltzmann brains disappear. It changes the ledger used to predict the observer's next experience. That is the comparison I want to pursue: a world that keeps its promises against one that needs a fresh coincidence for every promise kept.

The recipe is charged by its length, never by its cooking time.[^v1-c1-depth] A short recipe can therefore take an extraordinarily long time to produce a human. First an expansion that stretches a suitable patch nearly uniform, rather than a cosmos assembled one Planck-sized cell at a time. Then stars. Then evolution. Whether that route wins the full accounting remains part of my wager. But eons of cooking needn't appear as eons on the bill.

This gives the Anthropic Principle and Occam's razor different jobs, though they share a desk. The cost of locating yourself comes in three parts: a recipe for the laws, a recipe for the start, and an address inside the world they make. Occam favors short recipes. (Φ's interlude warned that a complete record of a plane's every turn still won't say where it took off; somebody has to supply the origin.) The anthropic condition filters the whole arrangement: laws and beginnings as well as addresses must permit the observer asking. So my rule is Occam wherever possible, Anthropic only where forced. Most of what we see should be cheap. Only what observers require should be rare, and no rarer than required.

Now tilt the picture.

What if everything (brains, vacuum, atoms, space) is a web woven from nothing sturdier than possibility? Not all possibilities, just the ones that can self-consistently compute themselves into being: a rare subset where mutual reinforcement lets a pattern stabilize long enough to call itself real. In short:

* We begin with a fabric: a possibility field.
* Expansion in that field creates volume: more room for configurations.
* For signal to form, you need repetition.
* For consistency to arise, repetition must be mutual.

In physics, the last line shows up as symmetry: the idea that certain transformations leave the underlying story unchanged. That "looks the same here as there" quality is part of what makes reality feel real.

## Alice, Down the Rabbit Hole: The View from Nothing

<!-- AI draft, 2026-11-12b: Alice reworked into three conjectures. The opening sentences, the waves passage, the rubber-band and Boltzmann passage, the confounder-holes paragraph and the closing paragraph are unchanged from the chapter; the previous Alice is kept in 01-chapter-relocations.md. -->
*The twist, in one line: if possibility is the floor, then energy may be its tilt, force its nudge, and reality its way of acknowledging itself.*

Alice followed a rabbit with a pocket watch, and the hole dipped so suddenly that she had no moment to think about stopping. I'm going to do the same, on purpose, for a few pages. The free lunch was an argument I'm prepared to defend at the table. What follows is further down: not established physics, and not yet an argument either. It's where the thesis goes if I keep falling.

**Shapes.** I imagine possibilities acquiring shapes, like waves, reinforcing or cancelling one another, and constraints selecting the patterns we measure. Quantum theory does something precise with interfering *amplitudes*, which are not ordinary probabilities. I haven't derived those amplitudes from bare possibility. The resemblance is an invitation, not yet a bridge. One thing I'll say now, because the next interlude builds on it. A possibility that is hemmed in can take only certain shapes, the way a string tied at both ends can sing only certain notes. Confinement can turn an open range of maybes into discrete notes, even when the list of notes is infinite.

I'll risk one reversal the textbooks don't make. They introduce the wavefunction as a device for calculating what particles will do. The first circle, coming up, will argue there are no particles to do it. So perhaps the wave isn't a tool for describing something else. Perhaps it is our first clear sighting of possibility itself, at a grain fine enough that it hasn't yet been averaged into things. Everything is a guess from in here. This one at least removes a middleman.

**Is energy possibility?** Physics will hand me a hint further on: for light of a fixed frequency, intensity tracks the expected rate of photon detections. Energy and chance meet in the accounting, without becoming interchangeable quantities. Here is the leap from the hint. Suppose energy isn't merely counted by possibility but is a feature of it: a tilt in the landscape of what's likely. Then a force would be a nudge down the tilt, and a particle wouldn't feel anything; it would be influenced. A rubber band offers a physical foothold: there are far more ways for its molecular chains to be tangled than stretched straight. In an ideal-chain model, the tension comes from that difference in available arrangements, rather than a change in the chain's internal energy. At fixed temperature, the outward force needed to hold it stretched is:[^v1-c1-entropic]

$$
f = -T\,\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial L}\right)_T
$$

This doesn't remove the molecules or their interactions; it shows how a force can emerge at another level of description. Ludwig Boltzmann's formula, carved on his tombstone in Vienna, ties entropy to the number of ways a state can be arranged:

$$
S = k_B \ln W
$$

For a system in thermal equilibrium with a reservoir at temperature $T$, the odds of its individual microscopic states are set by their energy differences:[^v1-c1-boltzmann]

$$
\frac{p_i}{p_j} = e^{-(E_i - E_j)/k_B T}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
E_i - E_j = -k_B T \,\ln\frac{p_i}{p_j}
$$

Read the second form from right to left and it says something remarkable: at a given temperature, an energy difference can be recovered from the logarithm of relative likelihood, multiplied by $-k_B T$. The relation already assumes an equilibrium ensemble and an energy scale; it does not conjure energy from nothing. Quantum physics has a cousin of this idea, in which a system's statistical state defines a dimensionless energy-like operator, the modular Hamiltonian,

$$
K = -\ln \rho ,
$$

and two physicists, Alain Connes and Carlo Rovelli, used it to propose that the flow of time itself comes from a system's statistical state.[^v1-c1-thermaltime] Whether every force is a nudge of this kind is a question physics hasn't settled; gravity, as we'll see, is the hardest case.[^v1-c1-entropicgravity] (Interlude 12 will pull hard on this thread.)

If the leap holds, a well in energy is a place where possibility pools, a bound electron is a standing wave caught in one, and an atom is a wave that has found its pool and settled.

**Acknowledgment.** How does one particle know another? Every electron carries the same charge and the same mass as every other, to as many decimals as anyone has measured. I don't think that is a property two electrons share. I think it is the absence of a distinction: swap them and you haven't made a new possibility, you've described the old one twice. So they don't know each other by sending word. They are one entry in the ledger.

My conjecture is that entanglement is how reality gets built. A wave can interfere; coupled systems can resonate and leave records in one another. I borrow those acts for what I call acknowledgment. Noise can resonate too, so the distinction I need isn't silence against noise. It is fleeting disturbance against correlations that persist and propagate. In a world with enough sameness, acknowledgment could spread; where that sameness is struck by a break, the pattern it throws (the way a grating throws a spectrum) is what we go on to call a thing. The second circle will supply a theorem about one such mechanism, not a proof of the whole guess. On this reading the universe is the part of possibility that has acknowledged itself all the way round. Physics owns several of the pieces: records the environment copies many times over, and space itself, in some models, stitched from entanglement.[^v1-c1-registration] What nobody has shown is that the pieces are one mechanism. That is the dare.

That leaves the oddities, the constants that look tuned and the beginning that looks impossibly tidy. I think of these gaps in our explanation as *confounder holes*, borrowing the word loosely: places where a pattern may be pointing toward something our account leaves out. A phone that pings every morning at exactly seven suggests an alarm. The universe is a harder diagnostic. We don't yet know whether its ordered start demands a deeper cause, follows from a selection rule, or exposes a mistake in how we count possibilities.

None of this is a theory. It has not derived quantitative predictions, let alone a test that distinguishes it from existing physics. It belongs among conjectures, not discoveries. It's a dare: the simplest story I could find for something rather than nothing. The rest of the chapter climbs back out of the hole and lets the physics answer. Curiouser and curiouser.

## Back Through the Fokker

Before she came to fetch me from my grandmother, and before we all boarded that Fokker together, my mother had spent most of a year tracing ghosts.

She was nineteen. She worked at Yale's high-energy physics lab as a scanner, one of the people who sat at projection tables for hours, looking through photographs of particle collisions for the events the physicists wanted. The film came from Brookhaven, which she described to me later simply as "an island," as if that explained why the particles there behaved like castaways.

The photographs came from a bubble chamber:[^v1-c1-bubble] a tank of liquid hydrogen held right at the edge of boiling, so that any charged particle passing through left a trail of tiny bubbles behind it, like a jet's contrail across a clear sky. A magnet bent the trails into curves, and the curve told the physicists a particle's charge and momentum. The neutral particles left no trail at all. You could only infer them from the gaps: a track that ended, and two new ones that sprang out of nothing a little further on, in a shape the scanners called a vee.

That, it seems to me now, is physics from the inside, in its purest form. Nobody has ever seen a particle. We see what one leaves behind in something else: a trace registered in hydrogen, photographed on film, circled by a nineteen-year-old with a pencil, measured by a physicist, argued over by theorists. Every step is one system carrying a mark of another. Even the invisible ones announce themselves by the shape of what's missing, like a basket you can't see from your seat.

She was good at it. She liked to remind us, with just the right amount of pride, that she had the highest efficiency in her group, and a little plaque to prove it. Precision, for her, was never an abstract virtue; it was a target she could hit and pin to the wall. Yet the symmetry of those tracks pleased her beyond the metrics. It gave her free spirit a kind of quiet charge, as if the universe had drawn a circle exactly the way she would have drawn it herself.

I was a toddler then, a long way from that island, in my grandmother's care and blissfully unaware of quarks. She was the one circling. And in the story I've told myself ever since, she was circling the places where nature revealed herself.


## The First Circle: The Birth of Order

Each roll of film unspooled the after-image of an impact: white scratches on dark emulsion, some two-pronged, some three, most of them routine. She'd circle the rare ones, the prize events, the little rebels that refused to fit the templates.

One of the rare ones had a name that would one day seem like a private joke between my parents: the **Ω⁻**, the omega-minus. A few years earlier, Murray Gell-Mann had looked at the bewildering zoo of particles pouring out of accelerators (pions, kaons, sigmas, deltas, each one announced as fundamental and each one a little embarrassing) and seen that they fell into families, neat geometric patterns of eight and of ten. He called the scheme the Eightfold Way; Yuval Ne’eman independently found the same organizing symmetry. One family of ten had a hole in it: a particle no one had seen, whose charge, strangeness and mass the pattern itself dictated. He predicted it, and at Brookhaven a bubble chamber found it, a short track that stopped and handed itself on, decay after decay, in a cascade that closely matched its predicted properties.[^v1-c1-omega] The missing tenth member was real, and so was the symmetry that had demanded it.

Later runs at Brookhaven produced more of them, and more of the film that came with them. If she did circle one, she wouldn't have known it was history. To her it was simply a crisp event, a good day's work, another tick in her efficiency column. In the story I tell, though, it's hers: the first circle, the one where the universe admitted that even its chaos obeys a syntax.

### The Grammar of Quarks

In 1964, Gell-Mann and, independently, George Zweig pushed the patterns a layer deeper. The families made sense if every one of these particles was built from a few smaller pieces, which Gell-Mann called quarks, borrowing the word from a line of James Joyce.[^v1-c1-quarks] A proton is two up quarks and a down; the Ω⁻ is three strange quarks, and that turned out to be a problem worth having.

Three identical quarks in the same state should be forbidden. The Pauli exclusion principle (which, along with electromagnetism, helps keep your hand from passing through a wall) says no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state. So physicists gave quarks one more property, a new kind of charge that comes in three varieties, whimsically called colors: red, green and blue. The three strange quarks remain identical particles, but their color states allow the combined quantum state to obey Pauli's rule.[^v1-c1-color] In other words, her particle helped force the invention of color, the charge behind the strong force itself. (Not bad for a scratch on film.)

<!-- AI draft, 2026-11-11. -->
Color then did a second job nobody had hired it for. A quantum theory with these gauge symmetries needs certain sums of charges to come out to exactly zero. An uncancelled *gauge anomaly* can wreck its consistency, including the conservation of probability. Other anomalies are perfectly respectable physics; this is the dangerous kind. Take one family: an up quark and a down quark, each in three colors, plus the electron and its neutrino. Add their electric charges:

$$
3\left(\tfrac{2}{3}-\tfrac{1}{3}\right) + (-1) + 0 \;=\; 0
$$

This is one consequence of the Standard Model's full anomaly-cancellation conditions. With its charge assignments held fixed, the books balance only because that leading factor is three. Change the other assumptions and the conclusion needn't follow. With three, the proton's charge cancels the electron's to the last decimal, and a hydrogen atom is exactly neutral.[^v1-c1-anomaly] Physicists call this a consistency condition. The possibility view takes the phrase literally: the parts have to agree before the whole can exist.
<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-11. -->

For a while, quarks were bookkeeping. Then electrons fired at protons at Stanford began bouncing off hard little points inside them, the way Rutherford's alpha particles had once bounced off the atomic nucleus, and the bookkeeping turned out to be real.[^v1-c1-dis] The force holding quarks together is carried by gluons, and the gluon made its own first clear appearance years later as three sprays of particles erupting from a single collision: a three-pronged signature, of all things.[^v1-c1-gluon]

The strong force has a peculiar temperament. Up close, quarks barely notice each other; pull them apart, and the energy stored between them grows, like a rubber band that only bites when stretched. Pull hard enough and the band snaps into new quarks rather than letting any one of them go free. No quark has ever been seen alone.[^v1-c1-asymfree] Confinement was the first social contract of reality: individuality forbidden, cooperation mandatory. It's also where most of the mass in your body comes from. The quarks themselves weigh very little; the bulk of a proton's mass comes from the energy of quark and gluon motion and fields.[^v1-c1-qcdmass]

Underneath all of this sits the idea that turned the particle zoo into a theory: particles are not tiny marbles but ripples in fields that fill all of space. There is one electron field, and every electron in the universe is a ripple in it, which is why every electron has the same intrinsic properties as every other, the fact Chapter Φ left hanging.[^v1-c1-qft] (Alice's version was blunter: swap two electrons and you haven't made a new possibility.)

### Why Symmetry Was Worth Trusting

A symmetry is a change that leaves something unchanged. Rotate a circle and its outline survives; move a well-isolated experiment, and the same relationships should hold. The point is not visual perfection but invariance. This made patterns such as Gell-Mann's worth investigating, and a mathematician most physicists had been slow to honor had shown just how much a symmetry could buy.

<!-- AI draft, 2026-11-12. -->
The machine underneath nearly all of this goes by one modest name, the action. For ordinary mechanical systems, take a possible path and add up along it the difference between the energy of motion $T$ and the stored energy $V$:

$$
S \;=\; \int (T - V)\,dt
$$

The path actually taken is the one where $S$ holds steady when the path is nudged a little:

$$
\delta S = 0
$$

Take a baseball. Quantum mechanics combines contributions from alternative paths with phases $e^{iS/\hbar}$. For a macroscopic flight, phases from neighboring, wandering paths largely cancel; near a stationary-action path they reinforce. The familiar parabola emerges in this classical limit.[^v1-c1-action] This is interference of amplitudes, not a vote of ordinary probabilities for whichever path looks easiest. What I want from it here is the grammar. A deep instruction in physics concerns everything the thing could have done, with a rule for how those alternatives combine. From baseballs to quarks, the laws are written in <!-- AI draft, 2026-11-14 (review of Codex pass). -->possibility space.<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-14. -->
<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-12. -->

Emmy Noether proved, while the world outside her study was tearing itself apart in the First World War, that a continuous symmetry of a system’s action—the quantity governing its equations of motion—comes with a conserved quantity. Laws that don't change from one moment to the next conserve energy. Laws that work the same here as over there conserve momentum. Laws that don't care which way you face conserve angular momentum.[^v1-c1-noether] Conservation isn't an extra rule bolted on; it's the receipt you get for paying with symmetry. Noether did this while Göttingen refused her a proper post because she was a woman. David Hilbert, who had invited her there, reportedly told his colleagues that a university is not a bathhouse.[^v1-c1-hilbert] She lectured for years under his name, and largely without pay.

The receipt has a fine-print clause worth keeping for later. An expanding cosmology generally lacks the time-translation symmetry needed for a single conserved total energy of the universe. Local conservation still holds; the trouble is extending that bookkeeping to the whole changing geometry. (Interlude 12 will collect on that.)

### Forces as Agreements

Then symmetry did something stranger than conserve. It helped dictate the form of forces.

The electron's wave has a phase, a position on a tiny clock face, and only differences of phase can ever be measured. Where to call twelve o'clock is a convention. Suppose we get democratic and let every point of space set its own:

$$
\psi(x)\;\to\;e^{\,iq\alpha(x)}\,\psi(x)
$$

Now nobody can compare the wave here with the wave next door, and comparing neighbors is what a derivative does, so the equations of motion stop making sense. The repair is a new field $A_\mu$ that keeps track of how the conventions differ from point to point, and adjusts whenever they are reset:

$$
D_\mu = \partial_\mu - iqA_\mu, \qquad A_\mu \;\to\; A_\mu + \partial_\mu\alpha
$$

That bookkeeping field is the electromagnetic *potential*: a connection for comparing phases. Its curvature, $F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu$, contains the electric and magnetic fields. Give the connection dynamics through the simplest Lorentz-invariant kinetic term, and Maxwell's equations follow. Local clock-setting alone hasn't manufactured light; the dynamical connection can carry it. <!-- AI draft, 2026-11-14 (review of Codex pass). -->Light is what that bookkeeping becomes once the connection is allowed to move on its own.<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-14. --> Color's three-way gauge symmetry similarly organizes gluons, and the electroweak symmetries organize the W, Z and photon.[^v1-c1-gauge] A force, on this reading, is shared bookkeeping that has acquired a life of its own.

Consistency even dictates who feels the force. In the Ω⁻'s own year, Steven Weinberg showed that a massless particle of spin 2 (the graviton, if gravity is quantum) requires a universal coupling to energy and momentum in his relativistic scattering framework.[^v1-c1-weinberg64] That gives Galileo's equal falling a remarkable consistency argument: gravity's coupling cannot play favorites among particle species. The same rule on every particle, everywhere: I take that sameness to be the smallest building block of reality, the point where a slab of possibility starts appearing consistent to itself.

And here I part company with Plato. Φ wondered whether the laws and the mathematics we describe the universe with might be things it produced, as a river produces its own banks. I think so. If mathematics were a set of perfect forms the universe has to run on, the fit between equations and bubble tracks would be the miracle Wigner himself once called it. If math is what a uniform enough patch gives off, the fit is no miracle at all. There are places where you can watch the banks form. Low-energy electron excitations in graphene obey a Dirac-like equation, with their own characteristic speed. The carbon lattice and electron hopping produce a law no isolated carbon atom possesses.[^v1-c1-universality] The details of a crowd can wash out while a few simple forms survive. Even counting needs interchangeable things to count, and the electron field is where nature gets them. Logic, the bare ban on contradiction, I'll grant comes with possibility itself. The rest (number, geometry, calculus) may be local produce: not the mathematical universe so much as the universe's math.

### What a Thing Is

Φ promised I would push an inversion to its limit: that things are what relations look like, once there are enough of them, from the inside. My mother's projection table is a fair place to push.

Start with the thing on the film. There are no particles, not in the sense the word smuggles in: no tiny beads with edges. Bohr's generation found that out the hard way. On the standard reading, an electron has no definite path between sightings; Heisenberg's relation forbids it a sharp position and a sharp momentum at once; and what the theory tracks is a wave of amplitudes. In quantum field theory, the field account I favor, particle counts describe excitations of fields. A quantum is what a detector, or a tank of hydrogen, can absorb in a transaction; its permitted energy still depends on the mode and the system.[^v1-c1-noparticles] The tracks my mother measured were receipts.

Then what is the energy being counted? Planck and Einstein gave the answer its shape:

$$
E = \hbar\,\omega
$$

For a component of definite energy, energy sets the rate at which its phase turns, priced in Planck's constant. Observable relative phases turn at rates set by energy *differences*; an overall phase has no clock we can read. Momentum sets a corresponding spatial phase gradient. Even rest mass enters this bookkeeping through $E=mc^2$. And Noether has told us when energy is conserved: when the relevant action has time-translation symmetry. My proposed reading is that energy measures how possibility turns over. Physics gives the phase relation; identifying what turns as bare possibility is my extra step. Confinement can select discrete energies, while unbound motion can retain a continuum.

One more piece clicks in here. For light at a fixed frequency, intensity goes as the square of the field amplitude. Divide an energy flux by the price per photon, $\hbar\omega$, and you obtain an expected photon flux. With a fixed detector, brighter regions give more counts. Born's rule makes a related, precise statement for a normalized particle wavefunction: its squared amplitude is a *probability density*.[^v1-c1-born] For position $x$ and a region $R$ in one dimension:

$$
p(x)=|\psi(x)|^2, \qquad P(x\in R)=\int_R |\psi(x)|^2\,dx
$$

The light example is a bridge, not an identity between energy and probability. Two matter waves can have the same position probabilities and different kinetic energies because their phases vary differently in space. That distinction is valuable to my wager: amplitudes alone are insufficient; the relations among their phases matter too. Alice took the hint further, asking whether energy and possibility might share a deeper origin.

![Two quantum states on a ring have identical, uniform position probabilities but different phase windings and kinetic energies. Real and imaginary parts of the wavefunction are shown by solid and dashed curves.](assets/01-chapter/phase-and-probability.svg){#fig-v1-c1-phase-probability width=95%}

*An exact small example. For a free particle on a ring of circumference $L$, $\psi_n(x)=e^{2\pi inx/L}/\sqrt L$. Equal position probabilities do not make the states identical.*

Now the table. Nearly all of an atom's mass sits in a nucleus a hundred-thousandth of its width. The rest is electron wave, about as thin a thing as physics has. So what stops the cup going through isn't stuff meeting stuff. It is two rules about relations: electron waves refuse to pile into the same state (Pauli again), and like charges push apart. It is a number with more zeros than this book has letters.<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-14. --> Solidity is a statistic over agreements. A table is less its atoms than the understandings among them.

And agreement needs the right kind of sameness. Alternatives interfere when their records do not distinguish them; leaving a distinguishable record can reduce or remove that interference. ## The Second Circle: The Fracture of Symmetry

The second circle is a symbolic one, and I'll be honest about why. The anomaly I'd like her to have circled was discovered at Brookhaven by James Cronin and Val Fitch, announced in 1964, the year I began my own journey as an embryo, but they found it with spark chambers, not bubble-chamber film.[^v1-c1-cp] A kaon decay can leave tracks; CP violation requires comparing decay behavior, not spotting one unusually eloquent vee. I don't know whether her particular films included those kaons. The connection I can draw is between her work of finding patterns and an experiment, at the laboratory supplying that film, which had found a pattern refusing to behave.

The heresy had started a few years earlier. Physicists had convinced themselves that nature was perfectly ambidextrous: every process had a mirror-image twin that worked just as well. Then Chien-Shiung Wu showed that cobalt-60 nuclei, lined up in a magnetic field, preferred to spit their electrons in one direction.[^v1-c1-wu] Parity, the mirror symmetry, was broken. The mirror lied.

Still, there was hope in the bookkeeping. If you flipped the mirror *and* swapped every particle for its antiparticle, said the theorists, the equations still balanced. That was CP symmetry, a kind of moral patch on the universe's crookedness, until Cronin and Fitch's kaons quietly violated even that. A tiny, statistically undeniable preference. The cosmic coin came up heads just a few too many times.

### A Universe with a Slight Limp

CP violation is physics admitting that its mirror has a hairline crack. In the Standard Model’s quark sector, it comes from a single complex phase in the matrix that describes how quarks of one kind turn into another, which Makoto Kobayashi and Toshihide Maskawa showed needs at least three families of quarks to exist.[^v1-c1-ckm] Philosophically, it's the permission slip for asymmetry itself. It tells us that the universe, like any living thing, limps a little. Its gait through time is not a perfect march but a slightly drunken stagger, and that stumble may be part of why matter survived (as for stress and boredom, I make no scientific claim).

Matter and antimatter can meet, bow politely, and annihilate in a flash of light. Yet there was a surplus: today there are roughly six ordinary-matter baryons for every ten billion background photons.[^v1-c1-asymmetry] To generate that surplus from a matter–antimatter-symmetric beginning, the familiar mechanisms need CP violation among other ingredients. Without some account of the excess, the stars, the planets, the carbon with opinions are all unpaid invoices.

Honesty compels a footnote to the poetry. The known quark-sector CP violation is insufficient to explain the whole imbalance; theorists are still hunting for the rest, perhaps among neutrinos, perhaps in some heavier field not yet cornered. The philosophical temptation is irresistible, even if the physics is unfinished: a slight departure from balance may have made room for everything that now has an opinion about balance.

If the crack is part of what tips the balance toward matter, then the extra families are no ornament. ### Broken on Purpose

The most consequential broken symmetry of all wasn't a crack but a choice, or what looks like one. At the enormous temperatures of the early universe, the electroweak theory describes electromagnetism and the weak interaction (the one behind certain radioactive decays) through an unbroken symmetry. As the universe cooled, a field that fills all of space, the Higgs field, settled into a state that didn't respect that symmetry, the way a pencil balanced on its tip must eventually fall in some particular direction.[^v1-c1-higgs] The unified electroweak description acquired two very different faces. The carriers of the weak force became heavy and short-ranged; the photon stayed massless and free to cross the universe.

Through their coupling to the Higgs field, electrons and quarks acquire mass. That means inertia, not friction: the field doesn't act as cosmic treacle, slowing particles down. The Higgs mechanism gives these particles mass without supplying most of the mass of a proton; the strong interaction does that. The Higgs boson was finally caught at CERN in 2012, almost half a century after the mechanism was proposed. Without that mechanism, atoms as we know them would not exist.

Symmetry breaking, then, needn't be a flaw in the code. It can be the act of writing itself: the equations allow several equivalent arrangements; the state takes one. For a storyteller, there's a whole world in that difference. Every life (mine, yours, hers) depends on prior distinctions that refused to cancel out.

The pencil has an equation. Start with a toy complex scalar field $\phi$, whose two real components let us draw the picture on a page. Its potential energy density depends only on its size, with $\mu^2>0$ and $\lambda>0$:

$$
V(\phi) \;=\; -\mu^2\,|\phi|^2 + \lambda\,|\phi|^4
$$

Nothing in that line prefers a direction, which is the symmetry. But the symmetric spot, $\phi=0$, is the top of a hill. The lowest ground is a circle at $|\phi|=\mu/\sqrt{2\lambda}$. A uniform vacuum selecting one point no longer has the full symmetry of the potential. The law stays symmetric; its chosen resting state doesn't.

A broken sameness also rings. Move outward from a chosen point on that circle and the field must climb; turn uniformly around the valley and its potential stays unchanged. Let the direction vary from place to place and gradients cost energy, but ever longer waves can cost ever less. For a spontaneously broken continuous *global* symmetry, the relativistic Goldstone theorem gives massless modes.[^v1-c1-goldstone] In a crystal, the related gapless modes are acoustic phonons: sound. Pions are nearly Goldstone modes, with a small mass because the relevant symmetry is only approximate. The Higgs is the crucial different case. Its would-be Goldstone modes supply the longitudinal polarizations of the massive W and Z; they do not remain separate massless particles. So a break doesn't only spoil a symmetry. It changes what can propagate. If I had to say in a line where new things come from: sameness, spread wide, then struck.

Here my two principles need careful bookkeeping. A symmetric law can be a short recipe; a particular physical outcome can require more information. A crystal's orientation relative to its surroundings is such an outcome. Merely picking a different gauge description of the Higgs is not: equivalent descriptions don't buy us extra worlds. Nor is every contingent choice anthropic. Observer selection enters when the outcome changes whether someone can be there to notice. The useful distinction remains: what the law supplies, what the history selects, and what our being here filters.

Hold on to that two-stroke: a cheap sameness spread wide, then a break that chisels it. Physics has it first. I'd be surprised if it were the last domain in this book to use it.

## The Weave of Reality

If the circles on her film were physics from the inside at the smallest scales, the sky offered the same discipline at the largest. Nobody has stood outside the universe either. We read it from traces that arrive here: starlight, radio hiss, the faint shiver of space itself.

It's tempting to picture the cosmos as a finished tapestry, a wall hanging stretched across infinity with our little drama stitched in somewhere near a corner. That picture is too tidy. The universe isn't a cloth woven once. It's a weave, ongoing and self-made, its pattern coming not from a loom standing outside it but from the recursive dance of its own threads.

In the old Newtonian picture, space and time were a pre-built stage, an invisible absolute grid where matter moved, like actors arriving after the set had been built. The twentieth century quietly burned the set. Einstein showed that space and time are one fabric, spacetime, and that it bends wherever there is mass and energy. The stage can move too, and responds to the actors; geometry and matter are coupled, though they are not the same thing. Mass tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells mass how to move.[^v1-c1-gr]

<!-- OVERLAP, candidate trim: the 1919 eclipse and LIGO recur in Interlude 12, Gravity is mostly time. -->
A famous early test came from the inside, of course. In 1919, during a total eclipse, Arthur Eddington (the same Eddington who would later name the arrow of time) photographed stars near the darkened Sun and found them nudged from their usual positions: their light had bent around the Sun's mass on its way to the photographic plate.[^v1-c1-eclipse] A century later, the LIGO detectors caught a gravitational wave from two black holes merging more than a billion light-years away. It stretched and squeezed four-kilometer arms of the detector by a minute fraction of the width of a proton.[^v1-c1-ligo] Spacetime itself, registering in an instrument.

Our observations give us no outside of this weave to look back from, and perhaps there was no "before" for it to start in. Chapter Φ already climbed that ladder (north of the North Pole, and all that), so I'll only add what physics adds: the weave is remarkably consistent. Gravity doesn't get moody from galaxy to galaxy. The speed of light is not a local custom. The electron doesn't renegotiate its charge when it changes postal codes. That sameness is what lets a telescope in Chile and a detector on Long Island describe the same universe. Relativity constrains which events can influence which others. That is a rule of causal structure, not a proof that all of reality is consistent. If the universe is a scam, it is at least a locally consistent one.

Gravity also sharpens the question of the cheap beginning. For a gas in a box, neglecting gravity, spreading out evenly is the probable state. With gravity in play, matter can clump into stars, galaxies and eventually high-entropy black holes. The early smooth distribution represents low gravitational entropy, even though its hot matter and radiation were close to thermal equilibrium.[^v1-c1-gravent] A smooth beginning is a short description without being a typical arrangement. I used to think those two ways of counting were quarreling. They are answering different questions: typicality asks what a given world will do next, and the recipe asks which world an observer should expect to be in. My wager is that on the second count the smooth start earns its place through a short recipe compatible with observers. Whether it is actually the cheapest such beginning remains a question for the census. Whether gravity itself can emerge as a statistical effect is a question several physicists take seriously and nobody has settled.

## The Cheap Engine

Across billions of light-years, the universe displays an astonishing sameness. The cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the early universe, makes the point: after accounting for our motion and foreground radiation, its primordial temperature variations are about one part in 100,000. In a plain Big Bang story, that's strange. Regions on opposite sides of the sky are too far apart ever to have exchanged a signal, even at the speed of light, yet they reached the same temperature, as if they'd compared notes behind our backs. That's the horizon problem. Its cousins are the flatness problem (why is space so close to geometrically flat?) and the relic problem (where are the exotic heavy leftovers that early theories predicted?). The universe looks as though someone ironed and decluttered it before the curtain rose.

The inflationary hypothesis, developed by Alan Guth, Andrei Linde, Andreas Albrecht, Paul Steinhardt and others, was both radical and almost embarrassingly simple: for a tiny sliver of a second, space expanded exponentially, driven, in common models, by a field whose potential energy dominates while it changes slowly.[^v1-c1-inflation] A tiny patch that *had* been in contact with itself got stretched to cosmic size, wrinkles and all ironed out. Inflation doesn't make anything outrun light; it makes space itself grow so fast that yesterday's neighbors become strangers who still share the same childhood conditioning. One short burst of space on cheap steroids solves the horizon problem, flattens the curvature and dilutes the relics.

This is what I mean by a cheap route, and the bookkeeping can be made explicit. Inflation is a loop instruction: double, and repeat. The scale of space grows as

$$
a(t) \;\propto\; e^{Ht}
$$

for an approximately constant expansion rate $H$; roughly sixty e-folds multiply lengths by $e^{60}$, about $10^{26}$. To see the problem at a specified epoch, look at last scattering, when the microwave background was released: regions separated by more than roughly a degree on today's sky had not had time to exchange signals in the standard, uninflated thermal history. Yet their temperatures agree astonishingly well. Inflation lets a sufficiently uniform, formerly connected patch grow to encompass those regions. Its ideal limit, de Sitter space, has as many spacetime symmetries as flat space: ten. That makes for a compact geometric description, not a claim of low thermodynamic entropy.[^v1-c1-desitter] Once suitable inflation gets going, spatial curvature becomes negligible relative to the expansion scale, driving the density ratio toward the critical value. And size comes cheap in the recipe: ten more doublings buy about a billion times the volume. Feynman pointed out that a chance fluctuation sufficient to explain only our local order gives us no reason to expect the same order farther afield.[^v1-c1-feynman] Instead the ordered evidence keeps arriving from across the sky. I read that extravagance as a clue that nature may be charged for recipes and not for stuff.

Thanks to inflation, our Anthropic corner of possibility space is saturated with repetitions. It's the region where things repeat just enough to be recognizable, but not so much that nothing ever changes. This seems to be the kind of place where spacetime, and eventually people with book contracts, emerge.

The economical description is real; the reading of it as a census of worlds is Standish's and mine. Inflation uses fields, energy and dynamics. It doesn't yet explain why those ingredients were available. Even a free lunch has a kitchen.

Ironing everything flat would be boring, though, and fortunately quantum mechanics never quite shuts up. During inflation, tiny quantum jitters in the field were stretched along with space into slightly denser and slightly emptier regions. The denser spots pulled in more matter, gravity amplified the differences, and over billions of years galaxies, clusters and the whole cosmic web condensed out of an almost featureless froth. Inflation is generous: within these models it gives you nearly uniform conditions and just enough imperfection to build stars, planets and the occasional teenager.

## Ghosts in the Gaps

Some of the universe's most important particles behave the way the neutral ones did on her film: you know them only by what goes missing.

In 1930, certain radioactive decays seemed to lose energy, as if the books didn't balance. ## Spooky Correlations

<!-- OVERLAP, candidate trim: Bell's theorem is introduced in Chapter 14 and treated in full in Interlude 14, Entanglement. A two- or three-sentence teaser may be enough here. -->
One more inside view, perhaps the strangest. In the same year the Ω⁻ turned up, John Bell, a physicist at CERN, found a way to test whether quantum correlations could be explained by local hidden instructions. Two entangled particles, sent far apart, can share correlations no such local model can reproduce, provided the experimenters' measurement choices are independent of those instructions.[^v1-c1-bell] Experiments, refined over decades, have confirmed violations of Bell's inequalities: the correlations are real, and they can't be used to send a message faster than light. Einstein had called the apparent connection "spooky action at a distance." The spookiness survived; the usable telegraph didn't.

For the possibility view, this is among the most suggestive results in physics. An entangled pair has a joint state that cannot be reduced to an independent state for each member. I hear a suggestion that relations belong deeper in our account than self-contained things. Bell's result doesn't choose that ontology for us; it makes the old separateness harder to keep.

## The Quantum Loom

All of this leaves physics holding two magnificent theories that refuse to marry. The Standard Model describes the particles and three of the forces through quantum fields, where uncertainty is the rule. General relativity describes gravity as geometry, smooth and certain. Each works superbly in its own domain, and together they break down exactly where both matter most: at the beginning of the universe and at the center of a black hole. Marrying them has been the longest courtship in science, and it hasn't produced an heir.

- **String theory** replaces point particles with tiny vibrating strings whose different notes appear as different particles, gravity included. It's elegant but extravagant: extra dimensions, and, in some landscape estimates, something like $10^{500}$ possible vacuum configurations, a landscape of candidate universes. The theorists call that a landscape; the rest of us call it feature creep.[^v1-c1-strings]
- **Loop quantum gravity** keeps the four dimensions and quantizes space itself into a network of tiny loops, so that areas and volumes come in discrete chunks. Some symmetry-reduced models of loop quantum cosmology replace the Big Bang singularity with a bounce from an earlier contracting universe. It's general relativity learning to speak quantum, with a thick accent.[^v1-c1-lqg]
- **Causal sets, triangulations and graphs** build spacetime out of discrete relations. Causal set theory treats spacetime as a vast set of events linked only by "this could influence that," with the slogan "order plus number equals geometry." Another proposal, quantum graphity, starts from a network of fully connected points that cools, like water freezing, into a low-dimensional spatial structure, proposed as a model for how space might emerge.[^v1-c1-graphs]
- **Spacetime from entanglement.** In some theories with holographic duals, removing the entanglement between two regions literally pulls the space between them apart. Geometry, in these models, is stitched together by quantum correlations.[^v1-c1-entspace]

None of them weaves the whole cloth yet. They disagree about the thread, but several pursue a possibility I find irresistible: spacetime may not be the floor, but something derived. Relations and quantum correlations have precise roles in some of these models. My broader possibility space has, so far, only a proposed relation to quantum theory: its structured state spaces might be constrained regions of that wider possibility. Why there are amplitudes at all is still owed.

Through that lens, the cosmos looks less like a machine than like an algorithm trying to verify itself, each layer of description arising from the need to stay consistent with the last. The cosmos is open source, but the compiler never stops running.


## Carbon and Company

The early universe was a poor chemist. In its first few minutes it made hydrogen, helium and a pinch of lithium, and then it got too cool and too thin to make anything else. Everything heavier, every atom of carbon in every living thing, had to wait for the stars.

Carbon had to squeeze through a narrow doorway. Inside a star, two helium nuclei can form beryllium-8 only for a fleeting instant, and a third helium nucleus must arrive before it falls apart. To account for the carbon we actually observe, Fred Hoyle argued that carbon must have an excited state that makes this three-way meeting far likelier. Experimenters found the resonance close to where he predicted.[^v1-c1-hoyle] The story is often retold as "we exist, therefore carbon must." The historical argument was about carbon's abundance; the anthropic moral came later. (Hoyle had another famous moment: in a 1949 radio broadcast, he'd coined "Big Bang" for a theory he opposed. The name outlived his alternative.[^v1-c1-bigbangname])

I like to imagine that one of those carbon atoms was me, or at least one of my potential selves: that the quark-and-gluon soup cooled into protons and neutrons, assembled into hydrogen, fused into helium, and after a couple of stellar lifetimes became the carbon that would eventually call itself *me*.[^v1-c1-b2fh] Forged in a stellar furnace, shed into space by a dying star, cooled into dust, and folded, after an improbable sequence of gravitational coincidences, into a planet that could ponder its own chemistry.

Along the way I had traveling companions. Maggie, the magnesium atom, was cooked in the hearts of massive stars, and would one day settle at the center of every chlorophyll molecule and hold life's energy currency together. Alex, the aluminum atom, had a radioactive cousin whose decay helped heat and melt the young asteroids of our solar system, so his family's first job was keeping planets warm; his own would be shedding heat from circuits.[^v1-c1-al26] And Silicon, my quiet twin, sat directly beneath me in the periodic table, with the same four hands for bonding, condensed from the same dust but fated for a different destiny: to conduct not thought but computation.

We are all born from the same stellar sermon. Some of us go on to think, others to compute, and a few, apparently, to narrate.

## The Hiss

Like all unpleasant creatures, I arrived with a hiss.

In the year I was conceived, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson began the measurements that would expose the universe's infancy. The two radio astronomers in New Jersey were only trying to measure radio signals cleanly, but their instruments kept hissing. They first scooped the pigeon crap; then they scooped Robert Dicke.[^v1-c1-cmb]

That same year, I got scooped too: not from nothing to something this time, but from material to alive. I packed up physics and moved into biology. My mother's real circles were still a couple of years ahead of her, but the circle that mattered most had already been drawn around a single cell. Its story belongs to the next chapter.

One rhyme before I go. Just as the protons and neutrons in my atoms had been reduced to up and down quarks, my biological blueprint was being reduced to four letters (A, T, C and G) and its working parts to twenty amino acids. Physics and biology had each, in the same generation, found that their whole bewildering variety was spelled with a very small alphabet.

## Turmoil in the Old Circles

After I left physics, the hiss kept talking, and what it said turned the old picture of the universe upside down.

It was the afterglow of the beginning, the light set free when the universe first turned transparent, stretched by expansion into a faint microwave glow at a few degrees above absolute zero. George Gamow's collaborators Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman had predicted something like it decades earlier, and almost everyone had forgotten.[^v1-c1-alpher] Its discovery settled a long argument: there really had been a hot, dense beginning, and the universe was not an eternal steady state. The theory Hoyle had named the "Big Bang" kept the name and won. The man who'd predicted the carbon in our bodies lost the argument about where that carbon's universe came from.

Then the hiss got sharper. A generation later, the COBE satellite found that it was not perfectly smooth after all: there were ripples in it, warmer and cooler spots differing by about one part in 100,000, patterns consistent with the seeds that inflationary models predict and gravity could grow into galaxies.[^v1-c1-cobe] A decade after that, the WMAP satellite mapped those ripples across the whole sky in detail, and a later mission, Planck, mapped them more precisely still. Reading the pattern of the ripples, cosmologists could read the universe's age, its shape and what it's made of.[^v1-c1-wmap]

And that's where the upheaval came. Within the standard cosmological model, the inventory said that ordinary matter—every atom of every star, every planet, every person—makes up only about 5 percent of the cosmic energy budget. Roughly another quarter is dark matter, inferred through gravity but not yet identified as a particle. The remaining two-thirds or so is attributed to dark energy, the name for what drives the accelerating expansion and whose physical nature remains unknown.[^v1-c1-darkenergy] The most precise portrait of the universe ever made from the inside turned out to be, mostly, a portrait of what we can't see. Even the pieces we measure don't quite fit: early- and late-universe estimates of the expansion rate disagree, and DESI's 2025 results, combined with other data, give model- and dataset-dependent hints that dark energy may evolve.[^v1-c1-tension]

My free lunch has something riding on the same hiss. Its smoothness points to a remarkably ordered beginning: a short description and a low-entropy state. Physics has no agreed reason those should have met. My recipe wager tries to account for their meeting, without pretending that a short description has already derived a low-entropy cosmos. The dark universe opens a larger hole in the account, mine included. Galaxy motions, gravitational lensing and cosmic expansion demand explanation; the best-fitting model assigns most of its budget to ingredients we cannot yet identify. The monster of existence turned out to be mostly made of darkness, and we found that out by listening very carefully to a hiss.

## To Carbon and Silicon

At a smaller scale, I (the universe that was now my body) started ballooning: from a single cell to trillions by the time I emerged from that literal cocoon. The carbon atom that had been, metaphorically, my physics entered through my mother's breakfast and became a tiny bead in one of those cells.

If the universe learned to repeat itself, what would it take for matter to repeat itself into memory? Perhaps every atom is already practicing, an electron clouding around its nucleus like a thought trying to remember itself. Someday, repetition will grow circuitry; the reflection will learn to hold its own shape.

Somewhere in that mirror, sand began to dream of permanence.
