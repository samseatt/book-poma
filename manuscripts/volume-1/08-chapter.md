# The Broken Banner

*Borders, Betrayals, and the Monster of 9/11*

*NATION*

> "The existence of a nation is a daily plebiscite." --- **Ernest Renan**, "What Is a Nation?" (March 11, 1882)[^v1-c8-renan]

The economy had carried me as far as it could. The invisible hand now pointed to something larger: the invisible border. The last chapter was about capitalism's grand casino; this one begins in its parking lot, where a quiet voice asks the question no quarterly report ever answers: *Where do you belong?*

And so, with a U-Haul full of ambitions and my sports car hitched behind it like a dinghy behind a barge, I drove east across the top of the country (Washington, Idaho, Montana, the Dakotas, my old Wisconsin) and crossed into Canada at Buffalo, leaving behind one castle of markets and entering another built of laws, flags and immigration queues. I did not know it then, but I had just stepped into the domain of nations: humanity's largest, loudest and occasionally least cooperative systems.

Crossing that border felt like driving through an emotional customs checkpoint. The officer asked the usual questions: "Citizenship? Purpose of visit?" I wanted to answer honestly: "Unclear. Existential. Possibly permanent." Instead, I murmured something about work and family while the Stars and Stripes faded in the rear-view mirror like a flag lowered to half-mast.

That was the moment it hit me: a border isn't a line on a map. It's a line right through your identity.

Toronto greeted me with glass towers, a north wind that questioned my life choices, and a faint smell of maple-flavored ambition. I took over my sister's basement in Pickering and rode the GO Train downtown, until one morning on the platform I literally froze my butt off, and my sports car's fat tires took to the snow like a figure skater on roller blades. So I moved downtown, within walking distance of Bay Street, Canada's polite imitation of Wall Street, where the traders still shouted, but only between apologies.

My job now was to help markets and banks bring their systems onto the web. It was familiar work, just translated into Canadian English, Canadian French, and Canadian fiscal caution.

I kept telling myself this was temporary --- an American on sabbatical in a colder timezone. My heart still thumped to the rhythm of Puget Sound; my dreams still wore flannel and smelled faintly of Starbucks. But belonging, as it turns out, isn't bilingual.

History itself seemed to be crossing borders that winter. I was still deciding whether I had any to hand.

## Possibility, Bordered

Chapter 5 found a society to be models that include each other, in bands near Dunbar's familiar, fuzzy scale of a hundred and fifty. A nation is the story that stretches the band to millions, as "A Band That Never Meets," below, describes. Like the economy, it runs on expectation; Renan, in this chapter's epigraph, called a nation's existence a daily plebiscite. Put the four questions to it.

*What is possible* for a person is decided, to a startling degree, by membership. A passport is a bundle of permitted futures: where you may live, work, vote and be protected. One economist estimated that the income level and distribution of the country a person lives in account for more than half of the variation in income in a worldwide comparison; and the same worker, with the same skills, can earn several times as much simply by moving across a border.[^v1-c8-membership] A nation also removes possibilities, which is what Hobbes, below, thought it was for. Over the centuries in which European states consolidated the business of violence, homicide rates fell from tens per hundred thousand people a year in the late Middle Ages to about one.[^v1-c8-homicide] The story opens futures for its members and closes some for everyone.

*What is cheap* is consent. A state can coerce people who refuse it; making every ordinary act depend on coercion is another matter. The United States has only a few police officers for every thousand people. Much of the work of keeping the law is done without an officer present. Fair procedures and a sense that authority is legitimate help explain that compliance, alongside habit, incentives and fear.[^v1-c8-tyler] That is Weber's legitimacy from Chapter 5, given its arithmetic. It is the cheapest glue a society has because the people it holds supply it.

*The sameness* is manufactured, as it was in the last two chapters, this time in people. The civic promise is to make citizens equal before the law and equivalent at the ballot box, one person, one vote, the way dollars were made interchangeable. To make that workable, nations also standardized their populations. In 1863, by France's own survey, roughly a quarter of its communes didn't speak French. Standard time zones, proposed by the Canadian engineer Sandford Fleming, were adopted by North America's railways in 1883, so that noon stopped being a local opinion. The philosopher Ernest Gellner argued that industry demanded it: a factory economy needs a mobile, literate, interchangeable workforce, and mass schooling in one language supplies it.[^v1-c8-standard] Chapter 6's sameness of parts required a sameness of people. The cost appears wherever a person refuses to fit.

*The break* is the border, a frozen accident with measurable effects. A line with no river or ridge beneath it, drawn by treaty, can produce discontinuities in life on either side. In a well-known study using 1988 data, one economist found that two Canadian provinces traded about twenty times more with each other than a province did with an American state of the same size and distance. Later work substantially revised the estimate and its interpretation; the border still mattered.[^v1-c8-mccallum] Prices, accents and marriages bunch at the line the way magnetic domains meet at a wall. My "zee," below, gave me away three times in a single phone call.

And confidence in a regime, like deposits in a bank, can be withdrawn. Regimes that look permanent can fall in a season, because many people hide what they think until enough others show it. Economists call this preference falsification, and argued that it's why almost no one foresaw the revolutions of 1989: each private doubt waited on a threshold, Chapter 5's threshold crowd again, until the thresholds tipped one after another and a decades-old order came down within months.[^v1-c8-kuran]

So the new answer. Chapter 4's person ran a model of itself. A nation is a society's model of itself, telling who "we" are, where "we" end and how "we" decide, written into law and drawn onto the ground. Its constitution, below, is the Ulysses contract of a people. And like a person's self-model, it can stay open to correction or close around itself, which is the business of this chapter's Alice and the next chapter's ideologies. On the ground floor, what was cheap became common. In biology, common was earned by copying; in a brain it arrived without happening; a person kept what never happened; a society made things real by expecting them; industry made the common cheap; an economy spent the future early. In a nation, a line that exists only on paper changes everything on either side of it.

## Seeing Like a State

James C. Scott called this *Seeing Like a State*: to tax, conscript, educate and police a population, a state makes it legible through surnames, property maps, censuses and identity papers. A census is a nation's memory, compressed. But the compressed picture always throws something away, and the state's errors fall hardest on the people who don't fit its categories.[^v1-c8-scott]

In this book's terms, a state is a classifier. It takes in the full, tangled reality of a person and outputs a label: citizen, resident, visitor, threat. Chapter 4 warned that a system checking its labels only against its own past labels closes its loop. A border is where that classifier meets you in person, at a booth, with the power to say no. What it owes the people it gets wrong is one question. Another is whether we have organized the categories around the wrong things.

## Alice, Down the Rabbit Hole: Who Drew the Container?

*The twist, in one line: perhaps the boundary of a governing body should be something its work keeps discovering, rather than an inherited line to which every kind of work must conform.*

Alice's neck grows until a Pigeon takes her for a serpent. Her explanation that little girls eat eggs does not rescue her; it enlarges the Pigeon's definition of serpent. A classification system can be remarkably adaptable when defending itself.[^v1-c8-carroll]

I will meet a version of that classifier at the border later in this chapter. But suppose the trouble begins before the officer, or even the form. We hand a nation one outline and ask it to contain a culture, an economy, a security system and a living piece of the planet. Then we spend centuries negotiating with everything that fails to stay inside. Perhaps part of what we call governing is the overhead of maintaining the wrong container.

The earlier rungs suggest a less obedient arrangement. A cell maintains a boundary while exchanging materials and signals across it. In a body, circulation, immune signaling and nerves coordinate different work through the same tissues. Our microbial companions make the picture stranger still: organisms with their own lineages participate in processes our bodies depend on. The organization is real, but its useful relations do not reduce to a single test of which DNA owns the address. Cooperation and conflict both live there; a microbiome has never signed a human-rights charter.[^v1-c8-living-boundaries]

What interests me is the way a consequence recruits a relation. Water supply might bind one set of communities; caring for an aging population, another. A language, a profession and the effects of an industrial process each draw different, overlapping shapes. These are natural relations in the sense of arising from the work and its consequences, not a plan to redraw every country along a mountain range. Nor should one relation swallow the others. A factory's gain can be a family's loss and a river's burden at the same time. No one of those accounts gets to declare itself the whole.

Multiple centers of government and overlapping jurisdictions already exist. Nations themselves emerged through bargains, migrations and conquest; they were never merely somebody's diagram.[^v1-c8-polycentric] The wager I want to pursue is whether governance could make its own boundaries revisable in response to what it learns. People would retain several kinds of belonging. Authority for a particular task would form around the relationships that task actually crosses, and would have to change when its consequences reached people it had left out. An exception could then be evidence against the container, rather than another applicant required to explain herself.

That is more demanding than decentralization. A hundred small tyrants can self-organize quite efficiently. We would need a way for people to contest the shape of a governing body without losing the rights it owes them, and for those carrying its costs to gain standing even when they cannot afford membership. Nor can every disagreement redraw the map. Some commitments need to survive the rearrangement. The hard problem is how to keep the arrangement capable of learning without making a person's protection contingent on its latest lesson.

I would like to find out whether such an organization could carry complexity more naturally, the way differentiated living systems coordinate without making every part negotiate every other part's work from scratch. This is an invitation to look for a mechanism, not a claim that biology has already solved politics. Its test would be whether a change in one place can be absorbed without exporting the burden to somebody left outside the count. Volume II will examine why our present arrangements so often fail that test; Volume III can try to give the alternative something to stand on. First, though, I would like the Pigeon to admit that the classification problem may not be entirely in my neck.

## The Invention of Borders

Before there were borders, there were boundaries, but they weren't lines on a map. They were rivers you didn't cross after dark, mountains that told your lungs to turn back, dialects that shifted over the next ridge, or the distance a horse could cover before it needed water, and so did you. For most of human history, geography, not geometry, decided where one world ended and another began.

Agriculture, surplus and storage changed that. Once grain could sit still, power could too. City-states like Uruk and Athens made identity civic rather than tribal: you were of this place, not only of this blood. Empires like Persia and Rome stretched authority across deserts and seas, but rarely along neat lines. Empires often governed through frontiers, zones where tax collectors, soldiers and myths thinned out toward the horizon. Sovereignty flowed outward until it met resistance, then tried again next spring.

The familiar European story gives this political space a birthday: the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, which ended the Thirty Years' War. Later generations made it the emblem of an operating system in which each state is sovereign within its territory and others must not interfere (in principle; practice was on back-order). The treaties themselves did not install that whole system at once.[^v1-c8-westphalia] Its developing logic helped sharpen distinctions we now treat as inevitable: foreign and domestic, citizen and outsider, and the peculiar idea that land acquires moral value simply by being labeled "ours."

Once borders became real, maps stopped being decorative and started being dangerous. At the Berlin Conference of 1884–1885, European powers set rules for their colonial claims in Africa without African representation. In 1916, British and French diplomats secretly carved up the Ottoman Empire's Arab lands in the Sykes–Picot Agreement, sowing instability that still sprouts on the evening news. In 1947, a line drawn in about five weeks by a British lawyer who had never visited India helped trigger one of the largest and bloodiest migrations in history.[^v1-c8-partition] These were not cartographic errors. They were moral shortcuts, committed with rulers and fountain pens.

Borders can be subtler than a checkpoint, too. When I moved to the suburbs east of Toronto, I phoned the phone company to set up a cell plan. I called my postal code a "zip code" and said "zee" for Z; she had also caught an American pronunciation in another word. When I told her I had just moved from the United States, she laughed: "You gave it away three times already." Same continent, same language, same brands in the strip mall. But a border all the same, enforced not just by customs officers but by tiny differences in vowels and expectations.

We live with a paradox: borders are intellectually critiqued yet fiercely defended. I've met very few people who think their own border is a bad idea and many who hope their neighbor's is.

## The Birth of the Citizen

If borders describe where a state ends, citizenship describes who counts on the inside. For most of recorded history, people were subjects, not citizens. Their relationship to power was vertical: they belonged to a ruler, a lord or a god, allegiance flowed upward, and protection, if it came at all, trickled down. Athens let some men (never women, slaves or foreigners) vote and speak; Rome extended its citizenship across an empire, though many lived under Roman rule without Roman status. The Enlightenment and the American and French revolutions reimagined citizenship as, at least in theory, universal, rooted in natural rights rather than noble blood. Theory moved faster than paperwork, and much faster than prejudice: women, colonized peoples, Indigenous nations and the enslaved remained footnotes in the grand story for generations.

Why should anyone accept a state's authority at all? The philosophers of the social contract gave the modern answers. Thomas Hobbes, writing after England's civil war, argued that without a sovereign power life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," so rational people would trade their freedom for security.[^v1-c8-hobbes] John Locke answered that people form governments to protect rights they already have (life, liberty and property), so a government that violates them forfeits its claim to obedience. Jean-Jacques Rousseau located sovereignty in the people themselves and their "general will." Max Weber, whose idea of legitimacy we met in Chapter 5, added the definition political scientists still use: a state is the organization that successfully claims a monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force within a territory.[^v1-c8-weber] Every word of it does work. *Successfully*: a state that can't enforce its claim is failing. *Legitimate*: force alone doesn't make a state. *Territory*: the line within which the claim holds.

As citizenship spread, it fused with a more emotional idea: the nation. The political scientist Benedict Anderson called nations "imagined communities": imagined because members of even the smallest nation will never meet most of their fellow members, "yet in the minds of each lives the image of their communion."[^v1-c8-anderson] Anderson traced how printed newspapers and novels, read simultaneously in a shared language, let strangers imagine themselves as one people moving together through time. A nation, in other words, is one of the scaling stories of Chapter 5, perhaps the most powerful of all, and like every scaling story, it compresses: one flag, one anthem, one history (after editing). Nationalism has liberated colonized peoples and unified fragmented ones. It has also turned minorities into problems to be solved and dissenters into traitors.

Citizenship has always been a grant of rights and a tool of control at once. Hannah Arendt, herself a refugee stripped of her German citizenship, observed that the stateless discover that human rights, supposedly owed to everyone, are in practice enforced only for those who belong to a state; what they've lost is "the right to have rights."[^v1-c8-arendt] I had already lost one home to ideological absolutism, long before any border guard was involved. The United States had taken me in next, and then mislaid me in its paperwork. By the time I arrived in Canada, I wasn't only looking for a job. I was shopping for a citizenship that could be trusted, for something less like a leash and more like a social contract --- still binding, but negotiable in daylight.

## Nationalism in the Twenty-First Century

If the twentieth century gave us the most flamboyant monsters of nationalism --- fascist states, imperial militarism, and post-colonial regimes where nationalism became a mask for dictatorship --- the early twenty-first gave us something subtler and, in some ways, more pervasive: **anxious nationalism**.

Many believed, briefly, that globalization had dissolved old loyalties. McDonald's and the internet seemed to promise that everybody would eventually eat the same fries under the same Wi-Fi. Instead, as migration, economic insecurity, pandemics and climate disruption crossed borders, and cultural change outpaced many people's sense of stability, nationalism returned --- now armed with talk radio, cable news, and social media.

Leaders from Trump to Modi to Orbán have experimented with a familiar formula: tighten the definition of "real" citizens, treat outsiders and critics as threats, and promise national renewal by shrinking who qualifies as "us."[^v1-c8-nationalism] This is nationalism as **policing the nation's essence** --- its race, religion, language, or lifestyle --- with growing ferocity.

For me, stepping from the U.S. into Canada meant more than swapping "zip code" for "postal code" and "zee" for "zed." It meant moving from a country that often defines itself by **exceptionalism**, to one many citizens half-jokingly define as **"not-America."**

On the surface, daily life didn't feel that different: same coffee chains, same spreadsheets, same software meeting the same deadlines. But under the surface, the **civic stories** diverged --- different notions of fairness, healthcare, guns, government, what counts as a scandal and what counts as Tuesday.

Citizenship, I realized, is less like joining a club and more like **installing a political firmware update**. It decides how your rights are structured, what your obligations look like, and which injustices you're expected to accept as "just the way things are."

## The Operating System of the State

If borders are a state's perimeter and citizenship its membership, its constitution is the source code. In theory, a constitution is a sober, rational document that defines the powers of government and the rights of the governed. In practice, most are written under duress, after revolutions, civil wars or collapses. They are what you get when a society wakes up from catastrophe and decides to put its trauma into clauses. Their most important function is as a commitment device: a way for a society to say, *even when we're frightened, angry or drunk on new technology, these are the things we promise not to do*. The trouble, as this book will find again and again, is that fear, anger and novelty have excellent lawyers.

Most modern constitutions borrow the same insurance policy, the separation of powers that the French philosopher Montesquieu described in 1748: divide the power to make laws, the power to carry them out and the power to judge them among different bodies, so that no one can do all three.[^v1-c8-montesquieu] Countries then wire those powers together in different ways. In a presidential system like the United States', the executive is elected separately from the legislature and the two can be deadlocked for years. In a parliamentary system like Canada's, the government is formed from the legislature and falls if it loses the legislature's confidence, which makes it more decisive and less checked. Both countries are federations, dividing power between a national government and provinces or states, but they lean in opposite directions: the American founders, wary of central power, reserved it to the states, while Canada's founders, having watched the American Civil War, gave more to Ottawa, and history has since pushed each toward the other.[^v1-c8-federal]

Democracy itself turns out to be mathematically stranger than civics classes admit. In the eighteenth century, the Marquis de Condorcet noticed that a group's preferences can go in a circle. <!-- AI draft, 2026-11-18. -->Take three voters: one ranks A over B over C, the second B over C over A, the third C over A over B. Two of the three prefer A to B, two prefer B to C, and two prefer C to A,<!-- End AI draft 2026-11-18. --> so that "what the majority wants" may not exist. In 1951, Kenneth Arrow proved something more unsettling: no rule that turns individual rankings of three or more options into a consistent social ranking can satisfy his full set of fairness conditions for every possible set of preferences.[^v1-c8-arrow] Every electoral system is a trade-off. The choice of system also shapes the politics it produces. The political scientist Maurice Duverger observed that first-past-the-post elections, where the candidate with the most votes in each district wins, tend to squeeze competition toward two leading candidates within each district.[^v1-c8-duverger] The United States fits the pattern almost perfectly. Canada, using the same system, defies it with several durable parties rooted in its regions, a reminder that laws of politics are tendencies, not physics.

Then there are the people who run the machine. Weber saw bureaucracy as the characteristic institution of the modern world: rules applied impersonally, by trained officials, through written files, efficient and predictable, and capable of hardening into an "iron cage."[^v1-c8-cage] A school of economists added an awkward observation: politicians and officials respond to incentives like everyone else, seeking reelection, budgets and influence, so government failures can be as systematic as the market failures Chapter 7 cataloged.[^v1-c8-publicchoice] Some of the most consequential moments in our lives are decided not by parliaments or presidents, but by an official with a stamp, a checklist and a little discretion. Governance is not only what a state says about itself in its constitution. It's how its agents behave when nobody is filming and everything is ambiguous.

Politics is both theater and plumbing. The theater is loud. The plumbing is decisive.

A veto can buy time to discover a mistake before it becomes law. It can also give a small, well-placed interest the power to keep a failing arrangement indefinitely. Capture occurs when the people a rule is meant to restrain acquire control over how it is written or enforced. Asking them to design the henhouse because they understand poultry so well is still asking the foxes. Deliberation and obstruction both take time; the difference is whether the delay permits a better decision or prevents any correction. Making everything faster would erase that distinction along with the delay.

## A Band That Never Meets

Chapter 5 found that human cooperation works most easily in bands of roughly a hundred and fifty, and that a fraternity, with its rituals, songs, lineages and sacred paddles, is a band engineered on purpose. A nation is the same engineering at the scale of millions. Look closely, and nearly every part of the fraternity reappears, inflated.

The rituals scale up first. Anthems, flags, national holidays, parades and the hush before a minute of silence are Durkheim's collective effervescence, the shared charge that binds a group, staged so that millions can feel it at once. The kinship scales up next. Nations speak of motherlands and fatherlands, founding fathers and fellow sons and daughters: the fictive family trees of the fraternity's big brothers, extended to people who will never meet. The costly signals scale up too. Military service, taxes, the oath at a citizenship ceremony and, at the extreme, dying for one's country are the hazing of a nation, and Chapter 5's communes predict their effect: what costs, binds. And there's always a rival across town, the Westwood of nations.

None of this happens by accident. The historian Eugen Weber showed that as late as the 1870s, much of rural France didn't speak French, didn't think of itself as French and rarely saw the state except as a tax collector. Schools, roads, railways, newspapers and universal military service turned "peasants into Frenchmen" within a generation.[^v1-c8-eweber] A nation is a band that never meets, held together by a story told in classrooms and barracks until it feels like blood. That's both its power and its danger. The same mechanism that lets millions of strangers pay for each other's schools and hospitals can also, when the story turns, let them see their neighbors as enemies.

## What a Nation Is For

Strip away the anthems, and a nation-state does a short list of things no smaller group can do as well. It keeps the peace, by monopolizing force so that disputes are settled by courts rather than feuds. It enforces contracts and property, so that strangers can trade. It provides public goods that markets underproduce: roads, defense, clean water, basic research, public health. It pools risk, through pensions, unemployment insurance and health care, so that bad luck needn't mean ruin. It makes collective decisions binding, through legislatures and elections. And it protects, or is supposed to protect, the rights of the people inside it, including against itself. Seen this way, a nation is the largest insurance pool and the largest agreement most people will ever belong to. Its anthem is the advertising; its function is the policy.

Why do some nations perform those functions well and others fail? Economists Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson, who shared the 2024 economics Nobel for their work on institutions, argued that institutions are decisive. *Inclusive* institutions, which protect property, enforce the law impartially and let many people take part in economic and political life, favor broad, durable prosperity. *Extractive* institutions, built to funnel wealth and power to a narrow elite, can produce growth while making its gains fragile and narrowly held.[^v1-c8-ajr] Their favorite example is a single city divided by a fence: Nogales, Arizona, and Nogales, Sonora, with closely related populations, shared climate and cultural ties, but different institutions on each side, and very different lives.

Acemoglu and Robinson later added a refinement that suits this chapter well. Liberty, they argued, survives only in a "narrow corridor" between two failures: a state too weak to protect people from violence and from each other, and a state so strong it crushes them. Staying in the corridor requires a constant race between the state's power and society's ability to check it, a Red Queen's race in which both must keep running just to stay in place.[^v1-c8-corridor] Volume II will meet the Red Queen again.

## Democracy, Under Stress

At the end of the Cold War, it was briefly fashionable to believe that liberal democracy had won for good. The decades since have been less kind. Organizations that measure democracy around the world, such as the V-Dem Institute, have recorded a long decline: by many measures, more people now live under autocratizing governments than under democratizing ones.[^v1-c8-vdem] The striking thing is how democracies now tend to die. Not with tanks in the streets, but slowly, through elected leaders who use legal means to dismantle the checks on their power: packing courts, capturing referees, harassing the press and rewriting electoral rules.

The political scientists Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt argued that constitutions alone can't prevent this. Democracies depend on two unwritten norms: mutual toleration, the acceptance that political rivals are legitimate opponents rather than enemies, and forbearance, the restraint not to use every legal power to its limit.[^v1-c8-ziblatt] When those norms erode, the written rules become weapons. That's Chapter 4's closed loop at the scale of a polity: a political system in which each side checks its story only against its own side's media, and treats every signal from the other side as hostile, stops updating, and starts fighting.

## New Machinery for Old Promises

Meanwhile, some places have been quietly redesigning how governance works. Estonia, a small country rebuilding itself after the Soviet collapse, decided to build its state in software. In 2001 it launched a secure data-exchange layer, X-Road, that lets government databases talk to each other, helping the state reuse information rather than ask citizens for it again; it introduced a national digital identity card; and today nearly every public service, from filing taxes to voting, can be done online.[^v1-c8-estonia] In 2014 it began offering "e-residency" to people anywhere in the world, access to digital business services without living in the country, not citizenship or a right to reside there. A state, it turns out, can be rebuilt as a platform, with all the gains and the new vulnerabilities that implies.

Other experiments target democracy's decision-making itself. In 2004, the province of British Columbia randomly selected 160 ordinary citizens, gave them months of study and asked them to recommend a new electoral system; it was one of the first citizens' assemblies of the modern era.[^v1-c8-bcassembly] Ireland later used citizens' assemblies to break deadlocks on same-sex marriage and abortion, which were then decided by referendum. In Taiwan, the civic technologist Audrey Tang, who became the country's first digital minister, helped build processes in which tens of thousands of people debate policy online through tools designed to surface consensus rather than amplify division, by showing participants the statements that people on opposing sides could both agree with.[^v1-c8-taiwan] The lesson running through these experiments is the one Interlude 5 drew for societies of agents: civics alongside control. Representative institutions stay, but citizens are brought back in, through random selection, deliberation and tools that reward bridging rather than shouting.

The year I arrived in Canada offered its own example. Nunavut's new legislature, created that spring, runs on what Canadians call consensus government: there are no political parties, members are elected as individuals, and they choose the premier and cabinet from among themselves, an approach rooted partly in Inuit traditions of decision-making.[^v1-c8-nunavut2] It's a small legislature for a small population. But it's a reminder that the party systems most democracies take for granted are one design among many.

## The Gaze of the State

Every new piece of machinery also extends the state's gaze. Hobbes imagined the state as a single colossal body, stitched together from its citizens. In the twenty-first century that Leviathan acquired fiber-optic nerves and a thousand algorithmic eyes. Estonia's digital state is efficient and, by design, transparent to its citizens, who can see which officials looked at their data. Elsewhere, the same tools can serve control. India's Aadhaar program has issued biometric identity numbers to more than a billion people, widening access to services and raising sharp questions about privacy.[^v1-c8-aadhaar] China has built extensive systems of surveillance and, in some cities and sectors, experimental "social credit" ratings, which are more fragmented than their reputation abroad suggests, but real enough.[^v1-c8-socialcredit] And Europe's General Data Protection Regulation, in force since 2018, tried to give citizens rights over the data states and companies hold about them.[^v1-c8-gdpr]

After September 11, the gaze sharpened everywhere. You stopped being only a person at a border and became a risk score flowing through a database. Long before "machine learning" became a buzzword, governments were feeding oceans of data into pattern-recognition systems, hoping the next attacker would flicker into view before acting. Some of it worked. But the cost was that everyone, all the time, became a potential misclassification away from trouble, and the burden of proof migrated from the state to the citizen, and then from the citizen to their metadata. Scott's legibility had found its ultimate instrument.

<!-- Recovery from pre-Fable canon, 2026-11-18; author requested restoration. -->
The strangest part? We helped build the cage. We carry tracking beacons called phones, tag our own faces in photos, and voluntarily upload our social graphs, location histories, and reading habits.

The Cold War feared microphones hidden in lamps. The twenty-first century buys them on sale every November and asks whether they're compatible with the smart doorbell.

Somewhere in that shift, the idea of "belonging to a country" picked up an add-on clause: *Subject to automated review.*

## Exit, Voice and Loyalty

What can a person do when a nation, or any institution, starts failing them? In 1970, the economist Albert Hirschman gave the answer that has organized the question ever since. You have two options: exit, which means leaving (switching brands, quitting a job, emigrating), and voice, which means staying and complaining, protesting, voting or organizing for change. What determines which you choose, he argued, is loyalty: the stronger your attachment, the longer you'll use your voice before you exit, and the more your voice will be heard because your exit is a real threat.[^v1-c8-hirschman]

Nations depend on that balance. Too little exit, and a state can ignore its people, as the Soviet bloc did behind its walls. Too much exit, and the people most able to fix a country leave it, taking their voice with them. Hirschman's framework also describes my own story better than any theory of nationalism. For years I'd had no loyalty to spend, because no nation had claimed me. I had used exit more than once. What I was looking for, without having the words, was a country worth having a voice in.

<!-- Recovery from pre-Fable canon, 2026-11-18; author requested restoration. -->
At its best, patriotism is affection scaled up: responsibility to people you will never meet, a willingness to pay taxes for schools you won't attend, roads you won't drive, forests you'll never hike. At its worst, it becomes xenophobia with better graphic design. A person can love a country and loathe its government; hold a passport yet feel more at home in a diaspora; care more about human rights or climate than about keeping one flag on top.

Every person with layered ties --- ethnic, religious, ideological, professional --- finds themselves juggling multiple "we"s: the nation of their passport, the nation of their parents, the global causes they care about, the digital tribes they roam with online. Which "we" is allowed to speak when the anthem plays? Which "we" gets to dissent? Which "we" gets investigated?

States tend to prefer single answers. Humans rarely come in that format.

## Empire and Aftermath

If borders, citizenship, governance and loyalty are the basic moves of the modern state, empire is what happens when a state keeps pressing all the buttons at once, and they still work far from home. Empires are gravity wells: capital, talent, attention and obedience fall toward them. Rome had roads and legions; Britain had gunboats and railways. Today's great powers have aircraft carriers, reserve currencies, app stores and content-moderation policies that quietly define the limits of acceptable speech across half the world. If that sounds abstract, check your pockets. Your phone likely speaks more of empire than your passport does.

The twentieth century told a comforting story about empire's retreat. Decolonization brought down old flags and raised new ones, and the world map filled with proud new nation-states. In 1945, the victors of the Second World War founded the United Nations on the principle that every state, large or small, was sovereign and equal; in 1948, its members adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which declared that every person, everywhere, has rights no government can take away.[^v1-c8-un] The two principles were in tension from the start. If states are sovereign, who may intervene when a state turns on its own people?

The year I crossed into Canada had forced that question into the open. In the spring of 1999, NATO bombed Yugoslavia for seventy-eight days, without the Security Council's approval, to stop the ethnic cleansing of Kosovo's Albanians.[^v1-c8-kosovo] Some called it the first war fought for human rights; others, a dangerous precedent of powerful states overriding sovereignty when it suited them. In the aftermath, a commission sponsored by Canada proposed a new principle, the "responsibility to protect": sovereignty is not only a right but a responsibility, and a state must protect its people from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity; when it manifestly fails, collective action may be warranted through the United Nations, under the Charter.[^v1-c8-r2p] That core responsibility was endorsed by the UN in 2005, and has been invoked, contested and ignored ever since.

Scholars of international relations still divide over what really governs the world of states. Realists hold that, with no world government above them, states live in a kind of permanent anarchy, and survive by power and alliances; liberals answer that trade, institutions and shared rules can tame that anarchy, and that democracies rarely fight one another. Both have had good decades and bad ones. Meanwhile, influence never retired; it rebranded. The age of overt empire has given way to what we might call API empire: systems of rules, standards, currencies and platforms that other countries must plug into if they want access to markets and capital. Opting out is possible in theory. In practice, it often means choosing to be poorer.

And empire doesn't exist only out there, in maps and summits. It exists wherever a person discovers that a distant pattern, a distant policy or a distant panic has quietly decided who they're allowed to be. Which is how this chapter turns, from the altitude of empires, back down to a single booth, a single officer and a single file.

## The Monster at the Border

I came to Canada as an exile from a country I'd never quite been allowed to join, and a very optimistic one. My apartment was in Toronto, my job was in Toronto, my pay stubs were in Canadian dollars (which my wallet appreciated less than I did), but some part of me still lived on Interstate 405. I checked Seattle's weather the way some people check on an ex: purely for meteorological closure.

But borders have an uncanny talent for telling you who you are before you're ready to admit it. My American immigration file had behaved like a bored raccoon: scattering documents, misplacing approvals and occasionally chewing the corner of a form for no discernible reason. I had done everything by the book, with two engineering degrees and years of exactly the experience the country said it needed, and still my employer and the immigration service had tossed my case back and forth like a diplomatic hot potato until time ran out. It was like a clinic that only knew how to treat the sick, faced with a healthy patient who had come in for a checkup, and determined to find him an illness to treat.

So I crossed north, was hired legally in Canada, and applied for permanent residence. The points system asked for seventy. I had ninety-eight. I said "please." They said "thank you."

Canada has a way of making even nationality feel like customer service.

## The Monster of 3/11

A year or so later, with my Canadian permanent residence in hand, it was time to change my default settings to Canadian: car registration, car loan, bank accounts, *HKEY_CLASSES_ROOT*, *HKEY_CURRENT_USER*, etc., etc., and, underneath it all, a real human life. I finally had a place on the planet where I could put my feet down without asking a bureaucrat "for how long?" With a place to stand, and confidence to match Archimedes', I felt ready to move the world, or at least to move *in* it.

"Hold my beer," said my newly minted neighbor to the south.

On March 11, 2001, I drove south to the United States for a few simple errands: to see my aunt, close a car loan and transfer a title, committing no crimes and violating no statutes, federal or otherwise. The border officer saw something else. Not wrongdoing; *pattern deviation*. Most people who looked like me and had moved to Canada that quickly did so for reasons border officers encounter daily. My life didn't fit the template. I lived in Canada and worked in Canada, yet I still had American credit, an American car and American attachments. To the officers, that wasn't the behavior of a legal immigrant; it was the behavior of someone pretending to be one. To me, it was a Sunday. They questioned me for hours, not out of malice but out of statistical habit. The system wasn't wrong about the general pattern. It was wrong about me. I was a black swan wandering into a pigeon classifier, the exception that makes machine-learning models cry, and they turned me back, unconvinced, like a misfiled book returned to the proper shelf. I wasn't a threat; I was a clerical anomaly. Yet again.

It was a shove I hadn't earned, from the country I had called mine for most of my adult life without ever quite being allowed to be its own. A supervisor later acknowledged the mistake. The next week, I flew out through Toronto's Pearson Airport, where a U.S. officer reviewed my stack of Canadian pay slips, nodded sympathetically and said "sorry," which is a big deal for an American immigration officer, even on Canadian soil.

## A Shift in Allegiance

By the time I was back in Toronto, something in me had shifted. It wasn't geographic; I was still living in the same apartment I'd been in for sixteen months. It was civic. That day, in my own mind, I handed the United States my resignation. Canada wasn't just where I lived; it was where I belonged. Not because it was perfect, but because the border had taught me something: sometimes you choose a nation, and sometimes a nation quietly chooses you back. I had spent my twenties fleeing ideological absolutism, dodging doctrinal minefields, and trying to outrun patterns that wanted to define me. Now I had found a country that measured people by points instead of paranoia, and I knew which flag I'd stand under when the wind blew hard.

I had a nation at last. What I didn't yet have was a creed to go with it, and that search belongs to the next chapter. But the resignation I'd handed in turned out to come with six months' notice.

## The Upheaval: The Monster of 9/11

Exactly six months later, on September 11, 2001, the wind blew very hard.

The country that had just shoved me away was struck by a shove it had done nothing to deserve, from the same kind of absolutism that had pushed me out of my first home, the kind that has a word, *apostate*, for people like me. Nearly three thousand people died on a single morning. The monsters I had fled in my youth (rigid ideology, absolutism wrapped in scripture, the certainty that kills curiosity) had followed me across two borders and torn into the world's most powerful nation. The shock wave didn't stop at the Hudson. It rippled through continents, alliances, markets and minds. For me, it was also an attack on *the project of modernity*: open societies, shared futures, the fragile dream that humanity might one day behave like eight billion adults instead of eight billion competing mythologies.

There were two monsters that day. The first planned the attack. The second was awakened by it: the reflex of empire, the machinery of suspicion, the fear that redraws borders inside people's hearts as quickly as it does on maps. Within months, the gaze of the state had hardened everywhere, and the pigeon classifier I had met at the border in March became the policy of half the world. I had run from one monster only to be misread by another, and now the two had found each other.

Every chapter in this volume has its castle and its monster. In this one, the castle was the nation-state: our grand attempt to shelter identity, rights and belonging inside human-made borders. The monster was the reminder that borders can wound as easily as they protect, sometimes through fanaticism, sometimes through empire, and sometimes through paperwork. Volume II will give the third of those a name: the tyranny of the pattern.

Before this book rises to its next chapter, on the stories beneath nations, it turns briefly to our silicon cousins. Machines carry our work across borders that can still stop their owners, and they've been arguing about how to govern themselves for as long as they've been talking.
